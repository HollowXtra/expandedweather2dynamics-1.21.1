# Expanded Weather2 Dynamics

NeoForge 1.21.1 addon for Weather, Storms & Tornadoes / Weather2. It adds expanded tornado dynamics, radar and anemometer blocks, severe-weather alert systems, SPC-style outlooks, damage surveys, supercell structure, and survival-safe storm damage controls.

*Ten thousand downloads. Thank you - and to everyone who has sent a report, a screenshot or a complaint: most of the sections below exist because of one.*

## Requirements

- Minecraft 1.21.1
- NeoForge 21.1.x
- Weather2 1.21.0-2.8.6 or newer compatible 1.21.1 build
- CoroUtil 1.21.0-1.3.7 or newer compatible 1.21.1 build

## Development

Build the mod jar:

```powershell
.\gradlew.bat build
```

Run the NeoForge client from Gradle:

```powershell
.\gradlew.bat runClient
```

Weather2 and CoroUtil are pulled from CurseMaven for the development runtime. The built jar declares Weather2 as a required NeoForge mod dependency and loads after it.

## Rendering mods (Sodium / Iris)

Foliage wind sway is a shader feature: the mod's built-in resource pack overrides `rendertype_cutout`
and `rendertype_cutout_mipped` so the sway can bend plants as they are drawn. That is a *vanilla*
terrain path. Sodium meshes chunks with its own shader programs, which no resource pack can replace, so
with Sodium (or an active Iris shaderpack) the sway is stood down for the session instead of being
half-applied, and one line is logged at client setup when that happens.

Because Sodium treats those two overrides as an error by default, the pack's `pack.mcmeta` declares them
under `sodium.ignored_shaders` - Sodium's documented way for a pack to say "these are deliberate and I
know they cannot apply to Sodium". Without that section the mod's pack is listed in Sodium's console as
incompatible. `.freebuff/scratch/PackMetaCheck.js` checks the file against Sodium's actual blacklist.

## Volcanoes

Weather2 grew volcanoes in 1.12.2 as a curiosity: a cone that filled its throat with lava, put a blob on top and set to stone. These run the eruption instead, and the ladder is the observed one - unrest, steam blasts, a sustained column, that column collapsing back down the mountain as a pyroclastic density current, effusive lava afterwards, and then the years of rain on loose ash that turn into lahars.

Every number behind it is anchored to something observed rather than tuned by ear: a VEI 4 column stands about 12 km and its collapse runs out about 12 km (the energy-cone rule), a VEI 4 leaves about 100 mm of ash at 5 km and thins as a power law from there, and a 5 m3/s lava flow reaches about 2 km. Each eruption's size is drawn from the volcano's own seed and episode count, so a volcano that has decided to go big stays big across a reload, and volcanic provinces are a property of the world seed, so the same ground is volcanic every session.

An eruption is a real hazard: it shakes the ground and brings blocks down, throws incandescent bombs across the flanks, lays ash downwind, makes its own lightning, buries and burns what a pyroclastic current runs over, and later runs as mud where rain meets fresh ash. It also feeds the existing alert products - an ash advisory, an ash warning close in, and a pyroclastic flow warning during a collapse - so the siren stations, the weather radio, the banner and the redstone output all handle a volcano without a second system. Growth, damage and the near-field block work are all budgeted and bounded by `volcano.hazardRadiusBlocks`; beyond it the eruption is represented by its cloud, its haze and its advisory instead of by blocks.

```
/ewd volcano spawn [intensity] [scale]     put one here (awake immediately)
/ewd volcano spawn <intensity> <distance>  put one ahead of you and watch it
/ewd volcano erupt <id>                    wake one up, or push it a rung up the ladder
/ewd volcano list                          every volcano, its phase, column and ash depth
/ewd volcano remove <id>                   forget one (its mountain stays)
```

All of it is in the `volcano` config section, including the switch for the whole feature.

## Rain-wrapped storms and virga

A storm's rain is a field, not a blob. `PrecipitationField` describes where the rain is as a function of position - a forward-flank core holding most of it, a rear-flank hook wrapping around the mesocyclone, a rain-free notch scoured out where the inflow is being drawn up, and weaker rear-flank and stratiform rain - and the cloud sheets, the sky shading and the flooding all read that one field. The consequence is the thing that was missing: a tornado can be visible from the inflow side through the clear slot while the same storm hides a second one behind a wall of rain, and which of those you get depends on where you are standing rather than on a property of the storm.

What "rain-wrapped" means here is measured, not declared: `SupercellStructureService.rainWrapShare` samples a ring around the vortex and reports what share of it is wet, so an HP storm raining hard on its forward flank while its tornado stands in clear air is not wrapped, and a moderate storm whose hook has closed around its funnel is.

The idea of asking a storm where its rain is, per position, comes from Weather2 Remastered's `IWeatherRain` interface (its `getDownfall(pos)` - full rate inside three quarters of a storm's radius, falling to nothing at its edge, with drizzle/rain/heavy thresholds at 50/200/300), which it then substitutes into vanilla's own `isRainingAt` so that the world's rain is the storm's rain. The parts of that worth having are the field and the single source of truth; the shapes here are this mod's own, built from the supercell structure it already draws. Remastered has no virga - that is ours, and it works off the same field: the thin margins of a rain area evaporate before its middle does, so the outside of a curtain frays into wisps while its core lands.

`.freebuff/scratch/PrecipitationSim.java` measures the field against its own claims - the core is wettest, the slot cuts real rain, the band stays joined to the core, the wrapped share separates a hooked vortex from a bare one - and each of those has been checked to fail when the field is deliberately wrong.

The field is now drawn as a volume rather than as pictures of rain. `RainVolumeShader` cuts camera-facing slices through a storm along the view direction and shades each fragment from the field at its own world position, so the rain has depth: flying into a storm shows parallax, walking around one shows a different storm from each side, and the curtain the hook drops between you and a funnel actually hides the funnel while the slot beside it does not. Terrain, structures and the funnel occlude it properly because the slices are depth tested; the sheets stand down while it draws, so the rain is never counted twice. It stands down in turn for classic look, for vanilla-rain mode and for an Iris shader pack, because a pack replaces the programs this one is.

The numbers it is tuned on are outcomes rather than tastes. From 150 blocks away the wrapped side of the hook leaves a funnel 74% veiled and the clear slot leaves it 6% - a twelve-fold difference, which is what rain wrapping is - and a look through 600 blocks of torrential core comes out 83% blind while a light shower over 256 blocks stays at 10%. Getting there took two fixes that only a measurement would have found: the virga term was taking a tenth off the bottom of every column even when there was no virga in the air, which is precisely the height every view of a tornado is made from, and the falling pattern was sparse bright threads in empty air, which the once-per-slice sampling randomised into 37% grain instead of rain. It is a veil with structure in it now, at 0.7% grain, and the modulation averages 0.927 so that density in the field means what it says on screen.

`.freebuff/scratch/RainVolumeSim.java` checks the interface between the three shader files, the tuning of the copy of the density function against `PrecipitationField` (it agrees to 1e-16), and what the rain hides. Every number in its copy of the shader has to appear in the shader, counted, so the copy cannot drift from the source while the checks keep passing.

The volume is drawn in the frame's own fog rather than in one of its own. `FogRenderer` has already chosen the fog by the time the rain is drawn - its colour comes from the sky, which this mod drives through the storm tint and the tornado's pressure fog, and its reach from the render distance, the player's effects and whatever a pack has done - so the program reads that back and the rain dissolves into the fog that is actually there: a tornado's collapsed whiteout swallows the rain the same frame it swallows the terrain, morning fog greys it, and a pack's fog applies to it as it does to everything else. The mod's own half is the mist a storm makes of its own air, on the terms that multiply rather than stack, so it vanishes exactly at zero - rain in clear air fogs no differently from anything else in it - and it follows the rain's own density, which is what keeps the clear slot a slot: the air there is 5.2x thinner than the core's, 0.088 of mist against 0.381 at sixty blocks.

What lights it is the sky over the storm, which is the whole answer in one reading: `getSkyColor` carries the sun's angle on the day - warm and full at midday, orange at sunset - the darkening this mod drives under a heavy deck, and the flash of a strike. So the rain cannot be lit differently from the sky above it. The floor on that light is applied by scaling the sky's colour rather than by mixing toward a grey, so a midnight storm's rain is dark blue at 0.22 of full daylight rather than flat grey. And because the sky already carries a strike's light on everything, the program adds only the difference between the light on a fragment and the light where the player is watching from: rain the strike is standing in flashes blue-white while the storm behind it barely moves, and standing between a strike and the rain it is lighting comes out as nothing rather than as a darkened storm.

`.freebuff/scratch/RainFogLightSim.java` measures both halves: the fog against an independent transcription of vanilla's own curve (exact to 1e-15 over four fog profiles), the mist vanishing at zero, the slot staying five times clearer than the core, the floor preserving a night sky's hue to 1e-9, and the strike's light landing on the rain near it and not on the far side. Seven deliberate breaks - the mist added instead of multiplied, the floor taken as a maximum, the strike fading to nothing, the strike's light made global, the pack path fogging twice, the mod setting the loader's own fog uniforms, and a uniform left unset - are each caught, with the tree restored byte-identical afterwards.

## Belts, lows and outbreaks

A synoptic low used to appear one of two ways, and both of them were a description of something else. A cluster of organized storms was declared to be one, and the "natural" path sampled the sky for a gradient at a random bearing from a random player - a score that was 14% storm count, so storms leaked into it either way. Storms are a low's weather, not its parent. Real lows come from the end of a chain that starts with uneven heating: warm air rises where the sun is strongest, returns poleward aloft, sinks again, and the boundary where the warm air it carried meets cold air is a baroclinic zone, which is the only place a low of this kind is born.

That chain is now in the world, as `ThermalCirculation`. Latitude is distance from the same equator line the hurricane/cyclone split already draws, so the two can never disagree about where the tropics are, and the belts run outward from it: a **rising belt** of warm, moist, sun-heated air that lifts; a **subtropical sinking ridge** where the same air comes back down, dry and suppressed; the **baroclinic zone**, which is the genesis corridor; and the **polar side**. The supply rises and falls with the sun over the day rather than sitting at a constant, and it is read off Minecraft's clock the way Minecraft's clock actually runs - tick 0 is six in the morning, so a curve read straight off the tick count would have peaked at dawn and been dark all afternoon.

A low is now born from that corridor and only from that corridor. Genesis places its candidate inside the belt - jittered across the corridor's own width, on whichever side of the equator line the players are, and along X wherever they are, because a belt runs east-west across the world and has no ends - and then asks the sky the same question it always did: how sharp is the boundary here, how much energy does the warm side have, how much moisture is either side of it. The storm-count term is gone and the rising belt's supply has taken its share, so a boundary the belt is feeding beats a sharper one that nothing is feeding. A cluster of storms can no longer father a system; what it can do is deepen one that exists, bounded, which is the feedback the old path was reaching for and had the wrong way round.

The belts also steer. Air converges on the rising belt and on the genesis corridor that feeds off it, and diverges over the sinking ridge, so the field adds a component to whatever is drifting through it - a low, or a storm on its way past. It is capped at 0.125 blocks per tick, about 5.6 mph, because the belts are a bias on a track and never the driver of one, and the two hemispheres mirror each other the way a real circulation does. Every boundary of the ladder is normalised so a wildly configured belt offset produces a cramped or lopsided world rather than a latitude that reports two belts at once, or a rung of the ladder that has nowhere to be.

A low then makes its own weather, which is the part that was missing: before this, the fronts changed what the day was forecast to do and no storm in the world was ever theirs. `FrontalConvectionService` draws a candidate point on one of the low's own front arms and asks the front geometry which regime that place really is, so a cell is seeded where a front is and not wherever the low's centre happens to be. The regime decides the cell, through `FrontalCellKind`: a cold front forces a line's own cells, capped at a thunderstorm; the warm sector between the fronts seeds discrete supercells, and they are the only cells here left their whole ladder; a warm front makes elevated, high-based, hail-bearing ones, capped well below a supercell. Capping is the point - an outbreak is made of warm-sector cells, and a cell seeded anywhere else must not quietly become one. A low has to have organised its fronts to force anything, it can only keep five of its own cells in the air, and each one is stamped so a low counts its own convection rather than the neighbourhood's.

An outbreak is its own kind of event rather than one violent storm happening to be drawn. `OutbreakService` gates one on extreme conditions and nothing softer: the day has to be issued at extreme or hyper extreme risk on the extension ladder - which is the environment's own verdict, past the significant-severe thresholds, rather than a second and more generous threshold invented for outbreaks - and a deeply deepened low has to have its warm sector over the same ground. The shape then follows the front the setup is on: a warm sector runs a corridor of discrete supercells, a cold front runs one long line, and the corridor is drawn on the low's own geometry, down the middle of the wedge for a family and down the cold front's arm for a line. Cells are seeded along that corridor as the episode runs, so an outbreak is a family stepping across the map on a clock rather than a point on it, and the episode is written down, so a world saved mid-outbreak comes back mid-outbreak with the same corridor, clock and count.

What a player hears of it is a product, not a broadcast. The advisory is a new alert level, ranked watch-strength - it never sounds a siren, never drives redstone, and can never outrank a real tornado warning - so it travels the same ladder as everything else and a player still only sees it with a station or a radio in range. The instruments name the belts from one shared source (`WeatherInstruments.beltLine`), which is what stops the barometer, the computer and the forecast readout standing in the same place from disagreeing about which belt it is, and the forecast readout and the computer also report an outbreak's progress while one is running.

`.freebuff/scratch/ThermalSim.java` and `.freebuff/scratch/OutbreakSim.java` hold it to those claims in 133 checks. The belt half runs the real ladder outside the game - ordered outward from the equator line, still ordered and still giving every rung a place when the configuration is hostile, the rising belt strongest at the line and gone one transition past it, the corridor peaking exactly on its own latitude, the supply flooring at night and peaking in the afternoon between dawn and dusk, dry air supplying less than moist and nothing supplying outside the belt, the score rising with each of its inputs with the supply worth exactly its share, the field converging on the rising belt and the corridor and diverging over the ridge, mirrored across the equator, and never pushing past its ceiling - plus the source claims: no cluster can seed a low, genesis samples the corridor, the belts steer, and only a warm sector's cells keep their ladder. The outbreak half runs the episode's own arithmetic - the corridor a segment with its ends clamped, an episode that knows when it is running and how much is left, seeding a cell spending one of the plan and stamping the clock, a save round-tripping identically, cells stepping along the corridor in order - and the source claims: the gate is the extension tiers and a deep low, the shape follows the front, the advisory is watch-strength and never ranks above a tornado warning, and its reach covers its own corridor and not the world. Thirty-eight deliberate breaks - the ladder's boundary moved, the layout no longer normalised, the belt's weight never fading, the corridor peaking at the equator, the supply read off the tick count so it peaks at dawn, the night floor removed, dryness made irrelevant, the supply worth nothing and then worth everything, the ridge converging, the corridor turned into a gale, the ceiling raised, the deepening feedback's rule removed, a live low no longer re-sampling, the candidate no longer held to the corridor, a low counting every storm near it, every front's cells keeping the ladder, a warm-sector cell capped at a thunderstorm, a low with no fronts seeding anyway, the per-low cap removed, the front geometry stubbed dead, the regime no longer deciding, any day starting an outbreak, no deep low needed, a warm sector running a line, cells seeded at the corridor's start, the corridor's length meaning nothing, the clock not stamped, a save losing its count, the engine not ticked, the advisory off the ladder, the advisory made as loud as a tornado warning, the belts not steering, the readouts given their own opinion, the forecast readout not naming the belt, and the outbreak's own defaults quietly changed - are each caught, with the tree restored byte-identical afterwards.

Two things are worth knowing in a world. The corridor is where lows are born, so a player deep in the tropics sees them arrive from the belt rather than form overhead - that is the feature, and `thermal_circulation.genesisBeltOffsetBlocks` (5200 by default) is where a world's mid-latitudes sit if you would rather they were nearer. And a really extreme day can now put a family of tornado-capable supercells in the air on purpose; the risk ladder, the outlooks and the storm gating are all unchanged, so this is the same environment made to behave like the day it is describing rather than a new source of danger.

## Where the weather happens: the ground decides the air

Every weather system in this mod already read one thing before it did anything: the air sampled around a position. That air was the same everywhere. A desert afternoon and a swamp afternoon were the same afternoon with different ground under it, the day's outlook was issued once for the whole world, and a storm that formed in a jungle was rated against the same sky as one over the plains. The most violent tornadoes in the real world happen where two airs meet - dry air aloft running over moist air at the surface, the boundary a dryline marks - and this mod had no way to describe that, let alone make it matter.

So the ground is read, and it decides. `BiomeClimate` classifies every biome - including every modded one - into one of twelve **families**: ocean, coast, desert, badlands, savanna, plains, forest, jungle, swamp, taiga, snowy, mountain. The name is read first, because that is the only thing that can tell a swamp from a forest or a badlands from a desert; then the numbers, so a biome nobody has seen is placed by its own base temperature and whether it rains. Heat comes from the biome's own temperature rather than its family, because a jungle in a cold world is a cold jungle; moisture and instability come from the family, because a swamp is wet whether or not it happens to be cool and dry air caps convection wherever it is.

The boundary is not an average, and that is the point of the whole thing. `contrast` is the gap between the wettest quarter of the samples around a place and the driest, over the gap that counts as a full dryline, scaled by how warm the place is - a dryline through a frozen belt is not a dryline. From the same four numbers a **belt** is named: temperate, dryline alley, warm wet alley, monsoon belt, marine air, dry belt, frozen belt, tropical coast. The belts nest in that order, so nothing is anything in a frozen country, the sea's own air is the sea's before it is anything else, a boundary is read before the uniform country on either side of it, and the dry belt is what is left of hot country with no moisture in it.

Four numbers then do two things. They feed CAPE and the storm mode index - the profile the storms themselves are built from, with the dryline worth up to 0.12 of the index on top of the loaded air it already describes - and they set a per-phenomenon odds table: the dryline's tornadoes, the sea's waterspouts, the desert's dust devils, the cold shower's funnel clouds. Measured at the shipped defaults: a desert is **1.911x** dust devils and **0.588x** tornadoes, polar country is **1.007x** snow devils and **0.382x** tornadoes, dryline alley is **2.172x** tornadoes, and loaded boundary-free marine air is **1.14x** waterspouts but **0.748x** tornadoes, because moisture without a boundary is not a supercell. The storm mode index for the same air is 0.770 without a boundary and 0.890 with one, and a fully loaded air is worth 4,420 J/kg of CAPE against a floor of 220 and a ceiling of 5,500.

Every term is written around one **reference air** - ordinary temperate plains country, half heat and half moisture, neutral instability, no boundary and no water - so that reference air comes out at exactly 1.0 for every phenomenon the land can make. That is deliberate: the mod's spawn chances were tuned as national averages, and a table that averaged 0.8 dust devils would have quietly thinned the world's dust devils by a fifth while looking like a regional feature. Writing the harness first is what found that - two rows were neutral at 0.8 rather than 1.0, and they now meet at 1.0 exactly, which the harness measures per phenomenon rather than in aggregate. Waterspouts and hurricanes are the two deliberate exceptions at 0.3 on land, because inland country really does almost never produce either.

It is all a reading, not a second weather system, and that is enforced structurally. The answer belongs to a **cell** of the map (1,024 blocks by default, sampled across a 192-block radius, cached for five minutes, 2,048 cells bounded, oldest out) so walking into new country is a handful of biome lookups and then nothing for minutes. `BiomeClimateService` is the only door: every spawn site, the daily outlook, the profile and the readout all ask it the same question, so they cannot disagree about what the air here is worth, and the strength knob (`climate.oddsStrength`, 0.75 by default) is applied once inside that door rather than at each call site. At 0 the physics still differs by biome - a desert is still a desert - but the map's own character is pulled back to neutral; off, the section returns the neutral table and the mod behaves exactly as it did before any of this existed. The day's outlook is also issued per region (`climate.regionalOutlook`), so the alley can sit at moderate while the desert next door is marginal on the same afternoon, and the forecast readout now names the air you are standing in: "plains air, dryline alley - odds strongest for tornadoes at 2.17x".

## The world's own calendar

The mod has always known the time of day - every storm reads the clock - and it has never known the date. Every afternoon in every world was the same afternoon, whichever week of the year it was: the same CAPE for the same air, the same tornado odds for the same ground, the same fronts whether a low arrived in April or in December. The seasons existed only as far as another mod happened to describe them, and with no such mod installed the year simply did not happen.

So the world counts days now, off its own clock: one day is 24,000 ticks, the day a vanilla clock calls a day, counted from the world's first tick and kept across restarts because it is the world's own tick count and not a session's. A year is however long the config says - 120 days is the mod's own compact year with ten days to a month, 365 is a real calendar year with a real day per game day - and a new world opens in the configured month, March by default, so a fresh save starts in its first spring rather than its first January. `/ewd season` prints where the world is.

The season then moves the air, and it moves all of it. **Spring** lifts instability and shear and the tornado odds with them; **summer** piles on heat and moisture while taking shear away, which is the pulse season - many storms, hard rain, few tornadoes; **autumn** puts the flow back and lifts the frontal convection a low makes; and **winter** takes the instability away, keeps the shear, leans hardest on the fronts and leaves almost nothing for a tornado to form in. Those four characters are the mod's own, written in `MeteorologicalCalendar.Character`, and what a world configures is how much of the season applies. The dial scales the season's departure from neutral rather than replacing it, so 1 is the shipped year, 0 makes every season behave like every other and 2 doubles how far each one departs from neutral - a world with the calendar switched off is exactly the world this mod had before any of it existed, which is the first thing the harness asserts.

Where it lands is deliberately one place each. `ThermodynamicProfileService` applies the season's temperature and moisture offsets to the air, and scales CAPE and shear before the storm mode index is taken - so every outlook, every storm, every panel and every instrument that reads the profile inherits the season without knowing it exists. `FrontalConvectionService` scales how readily a low's fronts make cells, which is what turns the same low into a November gale line and a July heat low with hardly a shower on it. And the tornado genesis chance in `DynamicStormController` scales with the season's own odds, which is where spring's spike actually happens. The hurricane window moved with it - late summer into early autumn, read off the same calendar - instead of the fixed 96-day cycle it fell back on when no seasons mod was installed; that cycle is still there for a world that has switched the calendar off.

With **Serene Seasons** installed, its season is read and used instead: a player running a seasons mod has told their world which season it is, and this mod has no business disagreeing. That mod's own temperature and moisture mapping still runs its season to the sub-season, because an early spring is not a late one, while the seasonal shape of instability, shear, fronts and tornado odds applies on top of it. Turn the sync off and the internal year runs under its own label regardless.

Modpack makers get their own year in two ways, and neither needs a screen. The `season_calendar` section carries a strength per season and a multiplier per month, in calendar order - 3.0 in May and 0.4 in December is a world whose storms belong to spring. And a datapack can drop `data/<namespace>/ewd_calendar/<anything>.json` into a pack:

```json
{
  "year_length_days": 365,
  "seasons": { "spring": 1.2, "winter": 0.8 },
  "months": [1.0, 1.0, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 1.2, 1.0, 1.1, 1.0, 1.0, 0.9],
  "windows": [
    { "from_day": 100, "to_day": 120, "multiplier": 1.4 },
    { "from_day": 320, "to_day": 40,  "multiplier": 1.3 }
  ]
}
```

Every field is optional, every figure is a multiplier rather than a value to be replaced, the config and the datapacks multiply together because they are two different people's dials, files merge in resource-id order so two packs can each contribute a hazard, and a window that ends before it starts wraps the new year - a winter running from November into February is one window, not two. Everything is parsed defensively: a typo costs that line, not the world's weather.

`CalendarSim` holds all of it to that in 181 checks, from inside the model and from the sources as text: the day and month arithmetic for a 120-day and a 365-day year including the rollover into year two, a real-length year's months summing to it with thirty or thirty-one days each, the season of every month, the four seasons asserted against each other rather than against constants (spring the most tornadic, summer the one without shear, winter the most frontal), a strength of zero, one and two with a season pinched past the floor instead of made negative and the temperature ceiling held, a wrapping window and two windows stacking on one day, the merge of two packs, every shape a datapack file can take including the malformed ones, and then the wiring: that the air scales CAPE and shear, that the fronts scale, that the genesis promotion reads the ground before it rolls and the season after, and that the command, the config section and the biome tag all exist. ## Tornadoes are born over plains country

Every other vortex in this mod belongs to the air: a dust devil is dry air, a waterspout is water, a fire whirl is a fire, and they form wherever their own condition is met. A land tornado is the exception, because it is the one of them that belongs to the ground - flat, open, loaded plains air is what it forms out of, and its own storm overhead is what it needs. It used to be able to form anywhere, which made a funnel a decoration on whatever biome the storm had drifted over: a tornado in a jungle, a tornado on a mountain, a tornado in the middle of a forest, rated and surveyed and drawn in full.

`TornadoGenesisGate` is now the single answer to "may a tornado be born here", asked at the moment a storm would otherwise be promoted to tornado-capable and again in the landspout path, because a landspout is a tornado - a high-based one, but one that puts a circulation on the ground and gets an EF rating. Waterspouts are not affected; they belong to the water they are named for. The rule itself lives in `TornadoGenesisChecker`, which is arithmetic with no game types in it, and there are two ways to be allowed: the biome reads as plains country through the same family classifier the air uses, so vanilla plains, sunflower plains, a meadow and a modded prairie all count without anybody listing them; or the biome is in `#expandedweather2dynamics:tornado_genesis`, which ships holding the vanilla plains biomes and is what a pack extends in a datapack when it wants a savanna, a badlands or an alien flatland in its tornado country. `tornado_genesis.plainsOnly` switches the whole rule off and restores the old behaviour.

Two honest limits. The gate is on becoming a tornado, not on being one: a funnel that wanders from the plains into a forest keeps its funnel, because the alternative is a tornado that dies at a biome border. A position the mod cannot read - an unloaded chunk - is allowed rather than refused, because a reading it could not take is not evidence that the ground is wrong. And the check is the ground under the genesis point, not the biome of the storm's whole region: genesis is a point, and this is that point. `/ewd season` prints the verdict for wherever the caller is standing, with the reason and the biome name, which is the quickest way to find out why a world has no funnels.

## Snow devils, and the three reasons there were none

The request was to fix the snow devil, and the report was that it does not spawn or dies instantly. Both turned out to be true, and they had three separate causes, none of which was the one thing the code looked like it was doing.

The first is the surface test. It accepted a snow layer, a block of snow, powder snow, or one of the three ices - and it read the biome's temperature against a ceiling of 0.28. What it therefore rejected was the ordinary case: most snowy country in this game is a grass block under a thin layer of snow, so the test passed only on the handful of surfaces that are solid snow, ice, or powder. A snow devil could only form on a frozen ocean, a glacier's edge and an ice spike field, and nowhere anyone actually walks.

The second is the weather. Both the spawn gate and the per-tick survival check read the level's own rain flag, and that flag is true whenever it snows anywhere, because vanilla reports snow as rain. In a snowy biome it snows much of the time, so a snow devil could neither form nor survive in the one biome it belongs to. What disqualifies one is water: rain, thunder, open water beneath it, and ground that is neither frozen nor cold enough to be frozen. Snowfall is the weather a snow devil forms in.

The third is that they had no entity. Every other whirl in the mod - dust devil, gustnado, landspout, waterspout, funnel cloud - is created as an entity by the spawner, and the client draws the vortex family's volumetric column from that entity every tick; a snow devil had none, so it had no column, and it also had no force on anything, because the force is the entity's own. It was an event on the server, a faint white smudge on the client, and nothing at all in between.

All three are now decided in one place, `SnowDevilGround`, which is arithmetic only and measurable without a world. The verdict for a point is one of seven - ground, snowing, too warm, no snow, open water, raining, thundering - taken in that order of precedence, and the precedence is the substance: thunder outranks everything, rain outranks the ground under it, and frozen evidence outranks the temperature, because a sheet of ice is its own proof that the country is cold even where the biome reports the sea's own temperature.

A living devil is judged by the same table, but over a **footprint** and on a **clock**. The ground is read at the centre and at the four corners of the vortex's own radius, and any one of them that is still frozen ground keeps it up - a snow devil that wanders off a drift and onto a bare patch is a snow devil doing what a snow devil does, and killing it on the first tick its centre left the snow is what made them vanish as fast as they appeared. It is read once a second rather than every tick, and it is not read at all before the devil's guaranteed life has run out, which ships at 200 ticks: ten seconds of existence that no ground check can take away. Between the two, `snow_devils.minLifeTicks` is the dial that makes the reported symptom impossible rather than merely unlikely.

The look is `SnowDevilVisual`, also arithmetic only, and it is deliberately the dust devil's *opposite* in the two things the eye reads first. Measured at the shipped numbers: at the spawner's own range - 18 to 46 mph at 1.4 to 5.4 blocks of radius - the column stands 22.6 to 37.4 blocks, against a dust devil's 42.6 at the same wind and radius; its base is 3.72 blocks wide against the dust devil's 5.04; and it turns at 22 degrees a tick against 32.4. It is topped out at 58 blocks and 4.6 blocks of width, cut into 14 to 34 layers, tapering from a base of 3.72 to a top of 1.12 with no widening on the way up - which the harness checks by walking the whole column rather than by sampling it. Its cloud is cooler and paler than anything else in the mod (0.90, 0.93, 1.00 - the renderer's earth fallback is 0.60, 0.50, 0.34), it has no dark core, and its opacity runs from 0.54 at the ground to 0.432 at the top.

It also stirs things gently rather than not at all, which is the physical claim rather than a hedge: a snow devil is a shallow, weak circulation over a surface that holds far less loose material than a desert, so its force is 0.34 of a dust devil's, its lift 0.62 of one, its suction box smaller, its wind capped at 58 mph, and its lifted debris has its own figure (0.12 against the dust devil's 0.55) instead of borrowing one. Three knobs are new: `snowDevilFairWeatherOnly` (off by default, and *on* is exactly the old behaviour - nothing falling at all), `snowDevilMinLifeTicks` (200) and `snowDevilStickDensity` (0.12).

## Chases: a record of what you have stood beside

The mod has measured a great deal for a long time and kept none of it. A 168 mph gust at forty metres scrolled past in the wind readout and was gone, which is a strange thing for a weather mod: everything it simulates is an event, and an event you cannot remember is indistinguishable from weather you did not see. So there is now a chase log - a per-player record of the storms you have been close to, printed by the weather computer and announced when a chase closes.

It keeps five things about each encounter, all of them extremes or counts rather than samples, because that is what a chase is: the storm's own peak strength while you were beside it, the peak wind your own position measured, your closest approach, how long you spent inside the core radius, and what the storm was called at its strongest. The two wind numbers are deliberately separate. The storm's peak is the one that earns the EF class and is comparable between chases; the wind at your position is lower than it unless you were in the core, and is the number a chaser would actually quote. Nothing here is a new model - the wind is the vortex's own field sampled at the player, through the same `WeatherPhysicalSnapshot.sampleAt` the anemometer, the radar and the damage survey already read, and the storm list is the same published snapshot stream. It is the same stream and not the same list, though: that stream also carries dust devils, fire whirls, snow devils, gustnados, waterspouts and funnel clouds, because a radar should draw them and a camera should photograph them, and a chase log may file none of them. It rates what it files on the published EF bands, which rate tornadoes, and it says a storm was chased; a whirl is not a tornado, a waterspout is not over anybody's land, and a funnel cloud puts nothing on the ground at all. So a chase is a tornado - the supercell tornado, its satellites, the floater and the landspout - and the spotter net's and the camera's own notes that the chase log excludes waterspouts are true of it. A funnel with no surface circulation also returns calm, and a calm sample carries an infinite distance, which reads as out of range rather than as a zero-speed chase; so the log does not decide what the wind is, it decides only what is worth remembering.

A chase is not a tick, and that is the whole reason there is a `ChaseTracker` between the world and the record. An encounter opens when a storm at or above the strength floor comes inside the radius, and it then stays open however much that storm weakens - riding a tornado down to a rope-out is a chase, and requiring the floor every pass would delete the second half of every one. It closes when the storm has not been seen for longer than a grace window, which is what stops a satellite circulation that dips out of the snapshot list for a moment, or a condensation funnel that lifts and returns, from being filed as three separate chases. The record closes at the last observation it actually had rather than at the moment the absence was noticed, because otherwise the grace window would be credited to the player as time beside a storm that had already gone.

Everything arithmetic lives in classes with no game types in them: `ChaseDraft` folds observations in, `ChaseTracker` decides what they mean, `ChaseLedger` counts them, and `ChaseLogReadout` turns a ledger into lines. That is also why the EF ladder moved out of `StormStageLabel` into `ChaseScale` and is now delegated to from there - the ladder was already shared by the survey and the damage tint, and a band boundary restated in a second place is a band boundary that drifts. The readout's ages are on the same clock as its durations, which is a small thing that would otherwise be a lie in both directions: a pass that lasted two minutes must not read as "2m" when it ends and as "3h" an hour later.

The ledger keeps its lifetime totals beside the capped recent list rather than deriving them from it, so a record never gets smaller the more you play: a player with a hundred chases keeps the last twenty-five of them and still has all hundred counted, with the strongest, the closest and the longest stay kept as whole records rather than as bare numbers - "an EF4 at 118 blocks, three days ago" is the thing you are trying to beat, and "168 mph" is a curiosity. The three extremes are asked for on the weather computer, which prints the summary, the worst wind stood in, the closest approach with its time in the core, the best chase with how long ago it was, and the last few storms newest first.

It is all switchable, and it awards nothing. The `chase_log` section has nine knobs - on or off, the sampling interval, the chase radius, the strength floor, the grace window, how many encounters are kept, how many lines the computer prints, whether creative players count, and whether a closed chase is announced. Creative is off by default, because a chase implies having been in danger and a creative player can stand in a core with nothing to lose; spectators are never counted at all. The log is a record and not a behaviour: it changes no storm, gives no advantage, and the numbers it keeps are the ones the instruments already read.

`.freebuff/scratch/ChaseSim.java` holds it to all of that in 130 checks. The ladder is checked on the number either side of every band boundary, in both directions - the class a wind earns and the tier a counter counts - including that a wind past the published top is classed `EF5+` but still counted on the top rung, because a count is about chases rather than about how far past the scale a wind went. The chase arithmetic runs for real: a worse observation never raises a peak, a farther one never lowers a closest approach, time in the core is counted in ticks rather than in samples, a narrow vortex still has a core you can be inside, a grace window of zero still keeps the tick it was seen on, an encounter closes at its last observation, and a storm is remembered as the storm it was at its strongest. The tracker is checked as a state machine - one storm is one encounter however many passes it takes, a storm seen this pass is never closed, one that was not seen is not closed early, a closed encounter is removed rather than filed twice, and a storm with no identity is refused. The ledger is checked on its extremes and on the one property the design exists for: capping the recent list never shrinks the record. The wording is checked from real tick counts, including the cases that would fail quietly - five seconds not rounding to "0s", a whole minute not printing a zero remainder, a day and a half not reading as thirty-six hours, and a sustained wind printing as a whole number without a locale adding a comma. ## The spotter net

Every alert this mod issues is built on one question - what has the mod itself observed - and that question has a blind spot the size of a funnel cloud. A circulation that is lowering but has not touched down is not a tornado to a scan, and it is unmistakably one to somebody standing under the wall cloud. The spotter net is that gap, filled by a person: fire a Spotter Flare at a circulation you can see and it becomes a real tornado warning on the same ladder as everything else. Sirens in range sound, radios hear it, villagers go under cover - and the mod's own warning takes over at touchdown, when it knows more than the report does.

The report is a sighting rather than a scan, and every rule follows from that. It has to be filed on a circulation you are near - 256 blocks by default, measured to the vortex itself rather than to the storm's centre - because a report is made from a safe distance and a chaser who has to stand in the inflow to report anything has the incentive backwards. The strength floor is 35 mph, well below the 65 the chase log uses to call something a tornado, and that is the point: a forming funnel and a satellite circulation are reportable, so a report can warn a village before the scan would have said anything. Nothing is spent when a report is refused - firing at the wrong cloud costs a flare and teaches players not to test the item at all - and a rate limit plus a cap on reports in force stops one player keeping a whole area under permanent alert.

What comes out of it is a tornado warning and nothing more. It is folded into `AlertService.highestAlert` after everything the mod observed itself, so an observed tornado always outranks a reported one of equal severity, and it is never a PDS or an emergency: a report says a tornado is there and does not claim to know how violent it is. It travels through the station network like every other product, which is what keeps the mod's rule intact - you still only see an alert you have a siren or a radio to hear, whether it came from a scan or from a person.

A report is also a threat, which is what makes a warned village survive. `TornadoThreatService` now includes the reports in force, with their own drift and width, and deliberately *not* through the wind floor it uses to decide something is a tornado - a report exists precisely to be believed below it. Everything that already asks for the nearest threat then treats a report as a funnel: the villager tornado drill shelters from it, the builder, lumberjack and surveyor stop working under it, and a tornado probe detects it. The spotter does not have to be anywhere near the village for that to work; the report is what is standing in front of it.

A claim that nobody can prove should stop warning people, so a report is believed for a fixed lifetime - a minute by default - and no longer. When the mod's own classification produces a tornado within 192 blocks of where the report said it would be, the report is proven: it stops warning anybody, the reporter is told what was observed and where, and the village that was warned pays a bounty out of its own chests. A village that cannot afford it pays nothing and does not go into debt, and the credit still counts - which is the same idea as the builder buying its own materials, in reverse: a village that survives a tornado is a village that can afford to be warned next time. A bounty earned while the spotter was offline is held rather than dropped, because a reward that evaporates when the server is not watching teaches players the loop is a lottery.

The part that stays is the rank, read off confirmed reports alone: Spotter at your first, Senior at five, Lead at twenty. The weather computer prints the record - filed, confirmed and lapsed, the rank and what is next, the crowd your reports have stood in front of, the bounty the villages have paid, and the last report with what became of it - and every transition is a message, including the one that matters most: how many villagers were in range when you fired the flare, and how many were warned when it was proven.

## Photographs

A weather mod ends up with a strange problem: it draws more than a player can attend to, and the thing that makes people stop and stare - a lowering wall cloud under a striated mesocyclone with the light going gold behind it - is exactly the thing the mod has no opinion about. So there is now a **Storm Camera**. Right-click photographs whatever the view is centred on and scores it; sneak and right-click opens the **Storm Album**, that player's ranked gallery, and the weather computer prints the best of it beside the chase log and the spotter record.

A photograph is worth up to a hundred marks in six parts, each with its own maximum, in `PhotoScore`: framing 25 for where the storm sits in the frame, proximity 20 for distance, subject 25 for the storm itself, structure 12 for the mesocyclone and wall cloud above it, atmosphere 14 for the light, and depth 4 for something in the foreground. Framing is the angle between where the camera points and where the subject is, measured in half-frames - 0 dead centre, 1 on the rim - and a subject past the rim is not a photograph at all and scores nothing. Dead centre is deliberately marked *down* (it keeps 86% of the marks) rather than rewarded, because a subject in the exact middle of the frame is the one thing every snapshot has in common; the comfortable band runs to 0.62 of a half-frame and the marks then fall away to the edge. Proximity is a band rather than a distance: full marks from 48 to 220 blocks, decaying to nothing at 480 and decaying towards nothing as the camera closes, because standing in the inflow gives the flattest light and the least information in a frame. Subject is the storm itself, in three readings no two of which are the same fact: half how strong the circulation is - the strength floor is also the zero of that score, so a dust devil below it is not a subject at all - then how much of it is visible, which is the difference between a rain-wrapped monster and a clear condensation funnel, then a fifth for how much ground it is working on. That last one is the debris: the damage swath it is cutting, so a rope tearing up a field and the same rope over clean grass are the same wind, the same width and different photographs, and a frame that shows trees and soil in the air is credited for showing them. It is deliberately a swath rather than a second wind reading, because repeating the wind here would count one fact twice and make this a quieter strength score; nothing is subtracted for a clean funnel either, so debris can only fill in a subject that is already there. Structure is read off the supercell geometry the mod already computes. Atmosphere is golden hour at both ends of the day plus a bolt in the frame, and the light's clock is turned into an hour of the day in exactly one place, because tick 0 is six in the morning and a curve read straight off the tick count would put sunrise at midnight.

Two of those parts fight over the same measurement, and that tension is the design. The line of sight to the storm is sampled along the subject's own height - nine points by default, the height taken as six times how wide the funnel looks, floored at 16 blocks and capped at 256 - because a two-hundred-block funnel is not one ray, and a single sample would call a storm visible through the gap between two trees. Up to 35% of the sightline blocked earns every depth mark: a little foreground is what gives a storm photograph its scale. Past that, the same number multiplies the whole score down, because what that foreground now is is concealment. One number cannot both reward and punish, so the two mechanisms are kept apart on purpose, and a score that paid depth marks for a subject nobody can see would be wrong in the direction that matters most.

Three exposures then scale the total rather than being part of it, because they are not mistakes in judgement but things in the way: how much of the sightline is blocked, rain between the camera and the storm, and being inside the tornado itself, which is a whiteout and the one photograph in this mod that cannot be taken. Being inside the core also suppresses the usual reading of obstruction, so a whiteout is reported as a whiteout rather than as a storm behind a very good foreground.

A score that only ever speaks after the fact teaches by hindsight: take the photograph, be told the framing was half out of frame and the storm was two hundred blocks too far. So a held camera now draws its own frame - a reticle with the rim of the view and the band that earns full distance marks on it, a mark where the storm actually sits, and two lines saying what a shot taken now would be given for framing and for distance. It shows exactly those two parts and nothing else, because they are the only parts that the camera's own aim and lens decide. The light, the rain, the foreground and how much of the funnel is behind a hill are facts about the world, and a viewfinder that guessed at them would be worse than one that does not show them.

The marks are not a second opinion. The reticle's framing marks are `PhotoScore.composition` and its distance marks are `PhotoScore.proximityFraction` - the very functions the shot will be scored with a moment later - and both readings come off the same frame geometry the scorer uses, so a subject the reticle calls dead centre is a subject the shot marks down for it. That is checked as the feature's central property rather than assumed: a target and a frame built from the same numbers must read the same marks, at the rim, at both ends of the distance band and dead centre.

Which storm the reticle is held against is the shutter's own rule, so the two can never be pointing at different ones: the candidates are filtered and chosen by the side that will take the photograph, and they are filtered there by the strength floor and the camera's range before being sent with the distance band and the range itself, every fifth of a second, to players holding a camera and nobody else. Turning the camera is measured on the client every frame - the aim is the client's - so the reticle is live rather than a fifth of a second behind it. The band travels with the packet rather than being read from the client's own copy of the configuration, because on a server those can differ, and it is the server's band the shot will be scored against. The pass is a tick and not a request for the same reason the candidate list exists at all: a client working it out for itself would keep a second, private list of storms, and a second list is one that eventually disagrees.

One honest note about its shape. The frame is drawn square because that is the frame the score measures - the marks come from a cone with one half-angle both ways - while the view rendered on a wide window is wider than that, so a storm the reticle calls off the rim can still be visible on screen. The reticle is what the marks will say, which is the thing it exists to tell you; the alternative is a HUD that agrees with the player's eye and disagrees with the score.

Which storm is in the frame is decided before any of that, by the picker rather than by the renderer: among the circulations the mod already publishes it takes the one nearest the axis of the view, breaking a tie on distance, with the strength floor inclusive on its own boundary and anything past the camera's range not a subject at all. The client sends the aim and its own field of view, because the server cannot know where a player is looking, and the server validates the player, does the raycasts and files the shot. Nothing is spent and no cooldown starts unless a photograph is actually filed, so pointing the camera at empty cloud costs nothing at all - that is a rate limit on the server's raycasts rather than on the player.

The album is the smallest third of it and the part with the fewest places to hide a lie. The best shot *is* the gallery's first entry rather than a separate field, because a stored best and a stored gallery are two answers to one question and a schema carrying both can be loaded with them disagreeing; the cap trims the *ranked* list, so a full album drops its weakest shot rather than its oldest; lifetime totals are their own fields, because the gallery is capped and the count cannot be recovered from it once it fills; and a shot's grade is derived from its total and never stored, for the same reason. Ranking is the total first, then the closer camera, then the stronger storm, then the earlier shot - the last two rungs are what make an album stable rather than arbitrary, so a photograph of a monster beats one of a breeze at the same score and two identical shots keep the order they were taken in. The verdict is explainable, because a score nobody can explain is a score nobody tries to beat: a filed shot is announced with its parts and then with at most two pieces of advice ordered by what the deduction actually cost, and a shortfall under 2% of the whole score is rounding rather than advice and is not named at all.

## The width of a funnel: surges, not a breath

The dynamic size was varied by a wave built out of summed sines and folded through one more sine, and a fold cannot make a wave stop being periodic. The width swelled and shrank on a beat, which is what reads as a tornado pulsing - and because Weather2's whole funnel is drawn from that one width (its own layer radii grow from the base size the mod hands it), every ring of it moved together, with the widest ring paying the most blocks for it, which is where the eye caught it first.

`TornadoWidthSurge` replaces the wave with a train of held levels. Each stretch of time has a width level hashed from the storm's own id and the stretch's index, the width is held flat at that level for all but the middle sixth of the stretch, and it crosses to the next level in between: about twenty seconds of movement in every two minutes, spent arriving at a new width and then keeping it. Every level is an independent hash of its index, so the sequence never repeats and there is no rhythm to lock onto, and two funnels on screen draw unrelated levels rather than surging together. It is still a pure function of (storm id, world time), which is what lets the server and every client agree on the width with nothing to sync and nothing to lose when a world is reloaded. The amplitude knob is untouched in meaning - `tornadoWidthTurbulence` (Width Surges in the config screen) selects how far from the calm width the funnel swings, and 0 is still the calm curve - and the damage path, the wind field and the scouring never moved with the wave and still do not.

`.freebuff/scratch/WidthPulseSim.java` replays the whole pipeline - the retargets, the wide-tornado floors, the easing and Weather2's own 0.01 blocks per tick radius slew - and measures the things an eye reads as a pulse: how often the width turns over, how much of its life it spends parked on a width, what cycle it keeps, and whether it returns to a width it has already had. The harness compiles `TornadoWidthSurge.java` and calls it, so every reading is of the code the mod runs rather than of a copy of it. Run against both models, the breath turns over 2.1 times a minute and parks 6% of its life; the surge turns over 0.6 times a minute, parks 53%, and holds the same amplitude (68% peak to peak around the same 12-block mean, so this is the shape of the motion and not how big it gets). The drawn funnel, which is what a player watches, parks 62% against the breath's 28%, and a surge's hardest second is 4% of the width - a ramp over tens of seconds, not a step. Measured on the raw class, 69% of whole seconds do not move the width at all, which the wave could not produce once.

Twenty-one checks cover it, including that the breath really did pulse (so the comparison is meaningful rather than rigged), that the mean stays on the calm target, that two storms correlate 0.05, that the same storm at the same tick always gives the same width, that the state reads the surge and no longer contains the sine wave at all, and - the one that closes the last gap - that the surge never returns to a width it has already had: its self-prediction at its own cycle is 0.00, while a width swinging between two levels on a beat would score near 1 and cannot hold its level, turn over slowly and be a pulse at the same time. Eleven deliberate breaks - the hold removed, the crossing squeezed into a hundredth of a stretch, the gain zeroed, a fast octave substituted, the slow octave muted, the storm dropped from the seed, the levels made periodic, the hash made storm-independent, a sine put back inside an octave, the knob no longer scaling the surge, and the knob replaced by a literal - are each caught by a measurement of the shipped class, with the tree restored byte-identical afterwards. `TopLayerSyncSim` re-run clean, since the top of the funnel is the ring the change had to be checked against.

One honest note: I cannot watch the funnel, so "does not pulse" is measured as turns per minute, time parked, cycle length and self-prediction rather than judged by eye. `TornadoWidthSurge`'s periods, weights, hold and gain are named constants at the top of the class if a surge should be slower or rarer than this.

## How big a tornado is allowed to get

The size of a storm used to be a memoryless draw: every width retarget rolled a fresh number out of the stage ladder, so a storm could be a rope at one update and a wedge at the next, a storm that had been enormous had no reason to be enormous again, and a storm could do its widest an hour after it had already peaked. Size was a property of the moment rather than of the storm, which is why the funnel felt arbitrary to watch and why "the tornado is so big" was really "the tornado is whatever the last roll said".

`TornadoSizeModel` keeps the ladder and drives it with three things that *are* properties of the storm. Its **temperament** - how big-boned it is - is hashed from the storm's own id once and never re-rolled, so a thin tornado is thin for its whole life and a monster is wide from the start; two storms of the same stage are now recognisably different storms. Its **life arc** widens as it organizes, holds through its climax and gives width back as it ropes out, tracked against its own circulation rather than against a clock, so a dying storm now contracts instead of staying the size it never earned. And its **environment** is the warmth, moisture and terrain reading the storm is already moving through. All three are pure functions of the storm and the world, so the server and every client agree about the size of every funnel with nothing to sync, and a reload cannot disturb it. The old draw is still there behind the same switch for anyone who wants it back.

The ladder itself is now geometric rather than linear, because the widths in this mod span three orders of magnitude: on a linear reach almost the whole range is spent on the top few percent of storms, and every ordinary tornado comes out the same size. On a log ladder an equal step of reach is an equal ratio of width - 12 blocks to 40 to 139 to 473 to 1609 - so the size classes fall evenly and "half way up the ladder" means what a player reads it to mean. The class bands are read by the radar's storm list, so a return is tagged a rope, a cone, a barrel, a wedge or a monster as it is listed.

How rare the big ones are is a dial, and the shipped value was chosen by measuring the population rather than by taste. At the value that was in the tree, the median storm came out at 263 blocks of damage path with **52% of the world's tornadoes a wedge or larger and 15% of them monsters** - a world of wedges, which is what the complaint had been about all along. The sweep in the harness picks the dial the model documents: at 3.0 the median lands at 81 blocks, monsters fall to 4.8%, and the top percentile is still a wedge at half a mile, so the big ones are rare rather than gone. The dial is measured on a one-mile ladder and on a three-mile one, because what "a tenth of the ladder" means depends on where the ladder ends.

Two smaller things were wrong and the measurements caught them rather than a player. The moisture reading had a constant floor in it, so the driest air the model could be handed came out at 0.38 and the dry half of the visible-share pair was **unreachable** - the thin condensation funnel over a wide swath that the feature exists to draw was a thing the arithmetic could not produce; every term is now a share of the whole and the reading spans its full range, from 0.35 of the swath in cold dry air to 0.75 over warm water in rain. And the size classifier returned NONE for a storm sitting at the bottom of the ladder, so the storm list printed no tag at all beside a real rope; NONE is now for a storm with no damage path, and a rope at the floor is a rope - which is also the band a storm passes through while it is forming and while it is roping out, so the tag has a colour of its own.

`.freebuff/scratch/SizeArcSim.java` holds 60 checks and compiles the class it measures through the source path, so every number is of the code the mod runs. The ladder is checked at both ends, at its geometric middle and through its own inverse; the population is checked for the median being an ordinary storm, monsters being the rarest band and the top percentile still being a wedge, at three different dial values so the dial is known to do something; a single storm's life is traced from forming to roping out and required to widen, to be widest at its climax, to come down by the configured share of its own potential - as the arc, and as the ladder image of that arc, which are different numbers and the check names both - and to sit at the bottom of the ladder while it is only forming; the rope-out is checked against the storm's own intensity; the moisture reading is checked to span its range and the visible share to reach both configured ends; the classes are checked band by band, including that a circulation below the floor is still a rope and that every band has a colour in the storm list; and 5,324 combinations of stage, temperament, arc and environment are swept to prove no reach and no width escapes its bounds. The wiring is checked as text: the state has to build its width from the ladder, take the funnel's share from the air it is standing in, hash the temperament from the storm, feed the arc its own intensity, dial the temperament through the setting, read every one of the five knobs, and let its width floors stand down only for a storm that is decaying or past its peak rather than merely because it is on the ground.

Twenty-one deliberate breaks - the ladder made linear, the temperament floor raised to the ceiling so every storm is the same size, the temperament no longer hashed from the storm, the arc's build floor at the ceiling so the arc becomes a second copy of the stage, the rope-out dropped, the rope-out ignoring intensity, the stage share flattened, rain no longer counted, a constant floor put back into the moisture reading, the visible share ignoring the air, NONE restored at the bottom of the ladder, the rope band collapsed, the temperament re-rolled per retarget, the dial replaced by a literal, the moisture reading taken as constants, the ladder replaced by a linear map, the arc fed a constant intensity, the lifecycle switch bypassed, the contraction trigger loosened, a width floor no longer standing down, and the default dial put back to the value the sweep rejects - are each caught by a measurement, with the tree restored byte-identical afterwards. `WidthPulseSim` and `TopLayerSyncSim` were re-run clean, since the surge rides on this width and the top of the funnel is the ring that pays most for it.

Two honest notes. I cannot watch the funnel, so every claim here is a measured distribution - a median, a percentile, a band count, a ratio of one regression to another - rather than a look at the screen. And the dial is a number in a config file: the shipped default only reaches new worlds and files that have not written the setting yet, so an existing config that already carries `tornadoSizeTemperamentSkew` needs it set to 3.0 (or deleted) to get the population above.

## How long a vortex lasts

A sub-vortex and a satellite tornado were each spawned with two numbers drawn independently: how fast it would circle its parent, and how long it would live. Nothing tied them together, so the worst combinations were born to die before they had been round even once. Measured across the shipped bands, the shortest-lived vortex the mod could make was a dust whirl with **0.83 of a turn** in it, an inner-core spot had 0.92, a suction vortex 1.05, a peripheral one 1.31, a hanging satellite funnel 1.11 - and a satellite tornado, drawn from its own slower band, 1.42 at the slowest rate its own speed wobble allows. A vortex that is gone before it has been once around its parent reads as a flicker rather than as a vortex, which from the ground is exactly "it dies instantly after one orbit".

`VortexLife` is the rule that ties them together, and it is one place rather than a number written into two spawners. A vortex is born with enough life to complete a minimum number of turns at the rate it is actually going to circle at, and the drawn life is kept whenever it was already long enough; the raise is bounded by the vortex's own band, so it can make a vortex live as long as it needs to be readable and can never make it live longer than its type permits. The satellite's rate is handed in at the *slowest* speed its own wobble takes it to, so the turns counted are turns it really gets rather than turns it gets at its best. Every orbiting type now covers at least 2.5 turns in its worst case, and the shortest-lived vortex the mod can make lives 61 ticks - three seconds - which is a thing in the air rather than a flash.

The second half of the same complaint was not about life at all. A satellite's existence was gated on its parent's *instantaneous* wind: the allowed count is read every tick from the storm's dynamic wind, and the code cleared the whole list the moment that count fell. The wind is dynamic by design and dips below a rung for seconds at a time, so each dip emptied the sky - satellites were dying mid-orbit with most of their life still owed to them, and the parent's wind on one particular tick was the real author of their fate. Now the count is only a **spawn** limit: a parent has to stay too weak for ten seconds before anything is taken back, and what is taken back is **retired rather than deleted**. A retirement pulls the vortex's own expiry in to a short fade, so the life curve it already had plays out and it leaves the way it was always going to leave - thinning, weakening, spiralling in toward the parent. A retirement travels to clients with the rest of the circulation's state, and a merged sync can only ever move an expiry earlier, so the client fades the same one the server gave up on. The one satellite that can still go instantly is the one the parent absorbs, which is the merge mechanic and not a cull.

The multi-vortex episodes had a version of the same fault in their own shape: when an episode shrank, every vortex over the new count was removed in a single pass, so four vortices could leave between two frames. The excess is now retired one per retarget interval, which is seconds apart, and each one is the client's own fade.

`.freebuff/scratch/OrbitLifeSim.java` holds 53 checks and calls the shipped classes rather than copies of them - the rails come from `SubVortexType`, the life rule and the cull decision from `VortexLife` - while the bands a vortex is drawn from are read out of the spawner's own source, because those are the numbers the question is about. Every orbiting type is measured across its whole life band against every rate it can be handed, in both orbit modes, and required to cover at least two turns and a half; the satellite band is measured the same way at its own slowest wobble; the numbers a player watches are checked too (the shortest-lived vortex lasts longer than the quickest full turn of its own type); and the cull is checked as behaviour rather than as arithmetic: the first weak tick remembers when it started and takes nothing back, one tick before the grace is still nothing, the moment the grace is up exactly the excess is retired and no more, the memory is continuous rather than restarting, a parent that recovers forgets its weakness, a parent that dies gives up every child even with room left on its own limit, and a retirement is a short fade that can never postpone an end.

Twenty-one deliberate breaks - the sub-vortex spawner no longer applying the rule, the satellite spawner doing the same, the satellite's rate counted at its best rather than its slowest wobble, the minimum turns set to nothing and then to a stingy one, the life rule ignoring the rate, the cap allowed to shorten a drawn life, a non-orbiting vortex given a life anyway, the retirement made an immediate cut, the retirement allowed to extend a life, the weakness memory restarted every tick and then never cleared, a dead parent keeping its children, the grace bypassed, the state clearing its satellites again, every keepable one retired instead of the excess, a retiring satellite counted against the limit again, the retirement not written to the wire, a merged sync not carrying the shortened expiry, the state cutting a retirement to an instant, and the multi-vortex episode thinning all at once again - are each caught by a measurement, with the tree restored byte-identical afterwards. `WidthPulseSim`, `TopLayerSyncSim` and `StormCentreSim` were re-run clean.

Two honest notes. The parts of this that live inside `DynamicStormState` cannot be instantiated outside the game, so those are checked as wiring rather than as behaviour: the breaks against them are written as removals of the wiring, because a check written against a line cannot see that line being ignored. And I cannot watch a funnel or a satellite in the sky, so "it goes round twice and a half now" is a measured count of turns, not a look at the screen.

## A squall line is the storms that make it, not a cloud this mod draws

The line's cloud was being drawn by this mod, and it was the wrong cloud. The server's half was already right - one gust front with a straight-line wind hazard, a rain curtain of its own, and a string of real Weather2 thunderstorm cells seeded along it, because a line needs storms in it to be a line rather than a rectangle. The sky over them was not: `ClientWeatherEffects` laid a hand-made field of shelf, lip, striation and scud puffs across the whole front, at every height from the cloud base downwards. That field is what roofed the sky over the player whatever the storm objects underneath were doing, and it is what read as one flat dark ceiling instead of a shelf hanging over a front. It is gone. What the line draws for itself now is its rain, and nothing else - `spawnSquallLineVisual` is the curtain and the water behind it.

Chasing it turned up something worth writing down. This mod's deck layer - the cauliflower, the hurricane shield, and the wall cloud this section used to describe - can only reshape a storm's *persistent* deck particles, `StormObject.lookupParticlesCloud` and `lookupParticlesCloudLower`. In the Weather2 build this mod is pinned to (file 6926103, the 1.21.1 release) neither map is written anywhere: the only bytecode that touches either of them is the assignment in `StormObject`'s own constructor and the read in `tickRender`. They are empty for the storm's whole life, so every reshape built on them has been a no-op in this version, and the wall cloud that was tried first never drew a single particle. The ceiling in the screenshot was the hand-made field above, and the fix is not another shape laid over the line - it is letting the storm objects draw the line.

So the line's shape now lives where a Weather2 storm's shape actually lives. `StormObject.tickRender` spawns a storm's deck - `size` puffs, one call in one loop - uniformly from a square of half-width `size` around the storm's own `pos`, at `pos.y + 30`, gated on the player being within 512 blocks and on there being no terrain ahead; the storm's own drive then snaps every puff flat on to `pos.y` within two blocks and drifts the mass with its own wind. That square is the entire silhouette: it is why a lone thunderstorm is a round mass, and why a line built as a row of thunderstorms came out as a row of round masses.

`StormObjectCloudDeckMixin` hooks the spawn that makes each puff - `spawnFogParticle` with the sprite, the one method every caller of the deck passes through - and for a cell the server seeded on to a line, draws the puff's x and z from the line's own axis instead of from the square. `SquallLineDeck` is the footprint as pure arithmetic; `LineStormDeck` reads the axis out of the synced line, re-applies Weather2's own two gates to the point that will actually be spawned, and refuses anything that is not at the deck's own height, so Weather2's ground fog and its funnel are left alone. Nothing else moves: the puff is still Weather2's own, spawned by Weather2's own loop at Weather2's own rate into Weather2's own list with Weather2's own sprite, render order, scale and lifetime, still snapped flat by Weather2's own drive and still drifting with the storm's own wind, and the height, the population and the puffs per tick are untouched. Every path out of the hook that is not a line calls Weather2's own method with Weather2's own arguments.

The hook is deliberately on the method rather than on a call site inside `tickRender`. The first version of this targeted the deck's call site by name and position, and on a real build that injection found nothing to match and took the game down with it before the world loaded - a call site is at the mercy of the compiler and a method is at the mercy of nobody.

The footprint is Weather2's own square stretched along the front and thinned across it: half a cell's size along the line, 0.62 of a size across it. ## Rain fog, and the mist that comes with the rain

Rain was the one kind of weather the mist was never going to cover, because the field was built not to. It is moisture: nearby water, the dew point, the time of day, the wind - every term in it is a reason air holds water *quietly*. Rain is the opposite of all of them. It was fed into the moisture model as one more term (half of its level into humidity, three quarters of it into how grey the air is), and then the wind and the clock multiplied the result: a squall line's 60 mph gust front throws that field at the 0.12 wind floor, noon throws it at the 0.34 daylight floor, and both together meant the two skies with the most water in the air - a line's curtain and a hurricane's band - were the two where you could see almost none of it. That is not a tuning mistake, it is the model being asked the wrong question.

So the rain brings its own air, from its own model. `RainFogModel` answers one question - how thick is the air a rain shaft puts around itself - and it answers it from two things. **How hard it is raining here**, which is vanilla's own world-wide level scaled by how much of the sky over the player is a storm's deck, so a plain shower in the open gets a third of its own level and the same shower under a supercell's rain core gets all of it. And **how deep in the shaft you are standing**, which is read from the weather this client has synced rather than from the level: a squall line's rain is a curtain *behind* its gust front, so the body the player is measured against is the line's own segment pushed back along the front's own direction by the same fraction of its radius the drawn curtain hangs behind it. A player a hundred blocks in front of the line is in the clear air it is about to eat; a player just behind it is in the wall.

The two airs are then composited rather than averaged: the mist is the thicker of the moisture one and the rain one, and the rain's is not multiplied by the wind, by the clock or by the temperature, because none of those three is a reason a downpour stops fogging the air. It also arrives at the rain's own rate - 1.5 seconds to nine tenths of its thickness, measured - where the moisture mist takes half a minute, so the fog cannot visibly lag the rain that caused it. Concretely, at the shipped defaults: a drizzle is a mass of 0.224 and a far fog plane of 168 blocks, a full shower is 0.391 and 84 blocks, and standing in a curtain takes it to the cap of 1.0 and the floor of 36 blocks, with the colour washed toward grey by up to 0.9 of the way and the golden light of a low sun suppressed by the same reading. Both numbers come from one mass, so the wisps at your feet and the fog in the distance can never disagree about how thick the air is - the rule the moisture field already kept - and the tornado whiteout and the pressure fog still win outright over both.

## Two tornadoes at once, and both of them real storms

The request was a video of a Weather2 Remastered outbreak - the chapter called "Twins" - and the thing in it this mod could not produce was two tornadoes on the ground at the same time. It had every way of making *one*: an outbreak already spreads a family of tornadic cells down a front, and a violent tornado can already grow satellites. But a satellite is deliberately not a peer - a capped fraction of its parent's wind, held only while the parent is strong, spiralling in to be absorbed - and it is drawn as a short mini-funnel, so a pair of equals never happened and could not have looked like one. What twins means is two tornadoes, neither of them the other's small brother.

This is the third time the same lesson has come up in this mod, so it was applied up front this time: **the pair is made of two Weather2 storms.** In the pinned build, `StormObject.tick` calls its own `setAndUpdateTornado()` whenever a land storm's stage is at or above forming, and that method builds a `TornadoFunnelSimple` through `setupTornado()` from Weather2's own tornado config - 150 blocks tall, a 10 block base, 18 of spin, 0.2 of radius per layer, a 120 block pull - and pins it to the storm's own ground position. A storm held at a tornado rung is therefore drawn, spun, sounded and felt as a tornado by Weather2 itself. `TwinTornadoService` seeds one beside a tornado that has already finished forming, keeps it on its pair's own rung while the pair lasts, and then gets out of the way. **This addon draws nothing for a twin**: no funnel, no particles, no layer geometry, and no client code at all - the service does not import `com.imbet.client` and never touches `TornadoFunnelSimple`, and the harness checks both as text so a later edit cannot quietly take that back.

The geometry is `TwinTornadoModel`, kept deliberately arithmetic: no Minecraft and no Weather2 types, so it loads and can be measured on its own. The second storm is set down **abeam** of the first - a quarter turn off the parent's heading - and 70 blocks **behind** it, so the pair stands beside each other and slightly in file rather than on a ruler line. Its heading is the parent's turned 4 to 13 degrees *back toward* the parent, so the two tracks run alongside each other for the whole life of the pair and close slowly, the way two circulations sharing one mesocyclone do. Its rung is the parent's own, one below it for the quarter of pairs that are not equals, never below the first tornado rung and never past the top of the ladder. Its size is 0.80 to 1.00 of the parent's. Its spin is the parent's own handedness, because two funnels turning against each other is the one thing that would give a pair away.

Measured at the shipped defaults: the pair stands 327.6 blocks apart - 320 abeam plus the 70 of trail - and over an eight-minute life at a storm's 0.03 blocks a tick, 288 blocks of travel, it closes 40.7 blocks, 327.6 down to 286.9. That closing is the weave and nothing else: at 8.5 degrees the pair's own drift over that run is 43.0 blocks, so what is measured is the design rather than an accident of arithmetic. The guard rail is the same geometry read the other way: the tracks would meet after 2,141 blocks at the shipped weave, and 519.8 blocks in the worst case the knobs allow - the floor spacing of 120 with the tightest weave of 13 degrees - both far longer than any pair lives, so a twin can never be steered into its own pair. How often one happens is the knobs as they ship: a 5% roll every minute of a formed tornado's life is eight attempts in eight minutes, which is 33.7% of long tornadoes and 9.75% of two-minute ones, so twins are a violent storm's sight rather than a habit. The ceiling on the spacing is Weather2's own 512 blocks, past which a storm's cloud deck is no longer spawned at all from where the first tornado stands: a pair has to share a sky and not merely a moment.

A pair is recognised without remembering anything. Every twin carries its own `spawnerUUID`, the same trick the addon's squall-line and frontal cells use, and Weather2 writes that string into the storm's own save data - so a twin is still a twin after a reload. Its pair is re-derived from the world on every pass as the nearest non-twin tornado, which is what keeps a reload from orphaning it. Everything downstream then follows for free, because a twin is a tornadic land storm and not a decoration: `DynamicStormController.isDynamicVortex` is simply a land storm at a tornado rung, so the wind field, the damage swath and ground scouring, the funnel's own build-in through `TornadoGenesis`, the radar signature, the warning, the spotter report and the damage survey all belong to it and to nobody else. Two tornadoes therefore mean two tracks on the survey, two warnings, and two separate paths of damage. What a twin is *not* is mergeable: two tornadoes converging is exactly what the addon's merge services read as a pair to absorb, so `SupercellMergeService.isEligibleSupercell` and both halves of `TornadoMergeService` skip a twin - the second tornado is never eaten by the first, and never eats anything itself. And it is not permanent: when its pair ends, or when its own life runs out, the addon stops holding it up and drops its ceiling to the hail rung, which is Weather2's own way of telling a storm to wind down, so the second funnel ropes out rather than vanishing.

It walks a whole pair's life tick by tick, at 0.03 blocks a tick for eight minutes, and requires the separation to close or hold on every single tick and never open, the twin to stay on its own side of its pair for all 9,600 ticks, the closing over that life to be the weave's own drift rather than something faster, and the pair to still be two funnels at the end of it. ## Simple settings

This mod's configuration is a few hundred numbers, and almost none of them are a question a player should have to answer - they are the tuning of systems, and they ship right. What a player does want to say is short, so the top of the config screen is now a `simple` section with nine dials: how often storms come, how many are kept going at once and within what range, how big tornadoes get, how violent they are, how hard it rains, whether they may break anything at all, how much the world's own seasons matter, and where a tornado may be born.

Each one is a multiplier on the detail rather than a replacement for it. At the default of 1 everything below is exactly the mod as it was tuned, and every other value scales that: 3.0 is a sky that is rarely quiet, 0.5 halves the wind of every stage, 2.0 draws a funnel and its damage swath at twice the size. The detailed sections keep deciding the shape of what comes - which environment makes what, how a supercell grows, which shape a funnel takes - so the frequency dial is a frequency and not a difficulty. Two of the dials have ends that are deliberately not symmetric: rain bottoms out at a thin veil rather than at zero, because rain drawn at zero is a thunderstorm overhead with nothing falling out of it, and destruction can only ever take damage away, because a master switch that could override the damage section's own would make that section a lie.

The interesting half was making one dial mean one thing. Three places were reading a wind the setting had not been applied to, and each is the same mistake in a different costume: the stage ladder's inverse - which decides what stage a storm's wind has earned - compared a half-strength wind against full-strength thresholds, so turning the dial down quietly stopped storms from ever earning a rung, which is the one thing the setting promises not to touch; the dynamic estimate blended a dialed band with the air mass's raw potential, so a violent tornado in violent air read half of its rung plus all of the air and barely noticed the setting; and the two vehicle/aircraft compat hooks used a raw reference as a denominator, which put the setting on the same gust three times over, since the force there is already proportional to the wind and scales with severity squared.

The ladder itself is now written once, in `TornadoWindField.rawBandCeilingMph`, with rungs rather than a cascade per reader: the ceiling of a rung and the floor of the next are the same number, so a reader that wants a band has to come through the helper, and everything inside the wind field's arithmetic is a raw wind that can be compared with every other raw wind in it. The dial goes on the two answers - the rung's ceiling and the dynamic estimate - and nowhere else, which is what makes half strength half of a storm rather than half of one of its terms. The live wind's ground-contact floor and the visual spin's reference winds carry it too, for the same reason.

Eight deliberate breaks - a band restated inside the ladder, the dial missing from the estimate's answer, the estimate bracketed by the dialed band, the inverse ladder raw again, the ground floor left at full strength, the spin's references raw, a compat denominator unscaled, and a dial on the same line as the band without being applied to it - are each caught, with the tree restored byte-identical afterwards.

The screen itself now opens on a page of those nine dials rather than on the full list. ## Hail, which had never landed on anything

This mod has grown hail since it grew a supercell structure: a hail core strength is computed every tick, a hail shaft is placed in the forward flank, the volumetric renderer whitens the downdraft with it and the radar paints the core's own dBZ. The ice itself has never hit anything. That is the whole of what changed here - a number the sky used is now a number the ground reads - and the ladder is the only input, because that is how hail is actually reported and because every consequence falls out of size rather than out of a separate switch.

The sizes, on the effective core strength after the storm's own precipitation character has held it down or let it grow by between 0.72 and 1.28: pea at 6 mm and 0.06, marble at 13 mm and 0.16, dime at 18 mm and 0.30, quarter at 25 mm and 0.46, golf ball at 44 mm and 0.64, baseball at 70 mm and 0.82. Marble knocks the heads off seedling crops and starts shredding canopy; a dime is the first size that reliably breaks a pane and the first that stings; a quarter - an inch, the threshold every storm report is written against - damages a bare head for a half-heart, beats farmland flat a quarter of the time and takes more than half a field, and still only breaks panes; a golf ball breaks glass blocks, takes two and a half hearts out of an unprotected skull and takes four fifths of a field; a baseball takes all of it. A helmet is protection rather than immunity: at the default 0.65 a baseball still lands for a third of what it would on a bare head, which is the honest reading of a cap in a hailstorm. Mobs take 60% of a bare-headed hit, because a herd has no helmet and does not put its arms up.

The pass runs every ten ticks off the storm's own structure and hits the entities in the shaft's footprint - 24% to 70% of the storm's radius, so 141 blocks across at a half core on a 300-block storm - that can see the sky, on a rate that gives a quarter an average hit every 45 ticks of exposure, a golf ball every 30 and a baseball every 18. Everything under a roof is skipped before the roll is even made, so a village that shelters takes nothing rather than a smaller amount. Blocks are read in a short column from two below the surface to four above it: the ground, whatever is growing on it, and the glass of a ground-floor wall, six columns and at most fourteen edits per pass.

## The leaves that come off the lawn first, and the doors that shut last

A gust front is visible at ground level before it is visible anywhere else, and what makes it visible is a handful of leaves coming off a lawn in a tight loop while the sky is still only grey. `LeafEddy` is that, and it has three conditions and no others: there is wind, there is a storm within 260 blocks that the wind belongs to, and there is something loose on the ground to lift. Strength is the product of the two rather than their average, so a gale with no storm in it lifts nothing and a still day under a supercell lifts nothing either - 0.675 halfway out at 130 blocks, and 1.0 directly under the storm's inflow at full wind. At most one spiral starts per tick out of six probed columns, it lives 20 to 64 ticks, turns 0.10 to 0.42 radians per tick around a circle 1.1 to 3.6 blocks across, and lifts one to three leaves per tick with between a quarter and 95% of that as genuine lift - so a weak eddy drags leaves along the grass, which is what a weak eddy does. The particle is the leaf block's own texture, carried by a falling-dust particle whose block state is the leaf block that was found, so a dark oak eddy is dark oak and a spruce eddy is spruce without the code knowing what a biome is, and the spiral is anchored to the litter under the canopy rather than to the canopy, because that is what swirls.

The villagers got the two behaviours their drill was missing. A villager that has got itself under a roof shuts the doors it can reach - 3 blocks at the edge of the warning and 9 as the funnel closes, inside 240 blocks and not before - and only ever closes them, never opens them, so the worst case of a wrong close is a house with its door shut, which is what the house wanted anyway. It also makes a noise while it is out in the open, every 150 ticks at the edge of the panic radius and every 50 at 40 blocks, pitched 1.05 to 1.35 and silent the moment it is sheltered, and it runs rather than walks while there is a threat at all.

Animals got their own drill, deliberately the opposite shape: a villager runs *to* something and an animal runs *from* something, and it bunches. An animal drifts toward the centre of the animals around it and stops once it is inside 6 blocks of it; with a funnel inside its 128-block reaction range it aims away from the funnel's *projected* position 80 ticks ahead rather than from where it is, blended 0.35 to the herd, so a herd leaves across the track rather than racing down it, and no animal ever aims inside 1.25 times the core radius from anywhere around it - inside the core, sideways is the only direction that is not standing still. Tamed animals are left to their owners, and water animals need no exclusion at all: they are not `Animal`s, so no goal is ever attached to one.

## The pass a wildfire leaves

A wildfire could burn a forest and leave no mark, and a burnt forest that looks like an unburnt one is a light show rather than an event. What a real burn leaves, in the order the ground shows it: the canopy is gone completely - a crown fire strips a tree in seconds, and the leaves are the part that burns - some trunks stand as black spars while others are only scorched, and the litter and the top of the soil are consumed. So the scar is not a new landform; it is the same forest with the parts that burn missing, which is exactly what `BurntPass` and `WildfireScar` write, once a second, over the disc the fire has already been through. The scar therefore follows the fire's track rather than ringing it, and has a direction by the time the fire has walked a few hundred blocks.

Heat is the one dial - 55% the cell's position in the disc, 30% the fire's own wind, a quarter extra where a fire whirl is working - and it sets both odds: a trunk chars 22% of the time in a cool burn and 85% in a hot one, the ground scorches 10% to 70%, and the canopy fate has no odds attached on purpose, because a burned forest with its leaves on is not a thing that exists. Up to 26 cells are read per pass within 85% of the fire's radius, each read 28 blocks up its own column.

A burnt trunk becomes `expandedweather2dynamics:charred_log`, a black pillar that keeps the axis of the tree it was, with textures drawn from arithmetic rather than a brush - grooved bark with the ash showing in the cracks, and an end grain with the growth rings legible in it. It is deliberately not flammable, so a scar cannot re-ignite and erase its own evidence, and it drops one charcoal when broken rather than dropping itself, which makes a burnt forest a small, deliberate charcoal yield and nothing more.

## Derechos, monsoons, and the two volcanic whirls

Three things a weather system is expected to have and this one did not: a squall line that is recognised for what it has become, a wet season, and the vortices a volcano makes. None of them is a new kind of storm, and that is the design rather than an economy: a derecho is a line, a monsoon is a date, and a lava whirl is a fire whirl with a different heat source. So each one is a *reading* that existing systems act on, and the work is in the reading and in the wiring.

**A derecho is classified, not spawned.** `DerechoModel` takes four measurements of a line - how long it is, how hard it is blowing, how far it has actually travelled, and how many storms are strung along it - and returns a shape (progressive, hybrid, serial) and a rung (C1 at 75 mph, C2 at 95, C3 at 115). The wind half of SPC's definition is kept exactly, miles per hour being miles per hour, and it is carried by the ladder alone so that there is one wind criterion rather than two that could disagree. The distance half cannot be kept: SPC's 400 km damage swath is 400,000 blocks, which is larger than this world, so the threshold is the shape of that criterion held to the scale of the thing being measured - 6,000 blocks, the largest distance a line here can actually cover, reached about fifty in-game minutes into a line's life and well inside the three to eight hours a common line lives. The classification ratchets: a droit is recognised once and keeps its name, because the swath it has already laid down cannot be un-laid by the line slowing down afterwards.

What a rung does is two hazards, and both are deliberately unlike a tornado's. A tornado takes things away; a derecho lays things down where they stand, which is the whole difference between the two kinds of wind damage on the ground, so a trunk goes over and its logs are laid along the ground immediately *downwind of the stump*, keeping the wood they were made of and taking the axis they now lie on - a forest a derecho has been through looks combed, not scattered - and the canopy above is left to vanilla's own leaf decay, which is what happens afterwards. The second is roofs, and it is why this is worth classifying at all: a derecho does not remove a house, it removes the *covering* of one - shingles, planks, slabs, wool, panes, carpet - and leaves the walls standing, and stone, brick, deepslate and metal are never touched, which is the reason a player can build against it. Both hazards go by the same 70 mph damage floor and the same ramp from the swath edge to its core, so a pass through the edge of one is gentler than a pass under it for the same reason on a trunk and on a shingle.

**A monsoon is a date.** `MonsoonModel` is a calendar first and a rain rate second: a season of `seasonDays` (8) inside a period of `periodDays` (32), with a trapezoidal ramp - two days of arrival, a plateau, two of withdrawal - and a phase taken from a property of the world so two worlds do not have their wet seasons on the same day. Where it lands is a reading of the mod's own air: the belt this mod already names gets the season whole, air that is merely wet gets it at par, and dry air gets just over half of it. It rains without any storm being responsible for it, in bands a few hundred blocks across placed on the season's own grid rather than around whoever is looking, and the coupling that makes it a season rather than a shower is that *the same rain floods harder*: at the peak in the belt a downpour floods 40% harder than it would in the dry season, because the ground it is landing on has been wet for days. All of it comes out of the calendar and the local air, which both sides have, so there is no packet and no chance of the client's grey sky disagreeing with the server's wet ground.

**The volcanic pair are the ground's vortices, not the sky's.** A lava whirl needs magma at a vent and spins up off the eruption's own intensity - 42 to 96 mph, 6 to 26 blocks wide against the crater that made it, 10 seconds to two and a half minutes of life - and it is *drawn in its own light* rather than tinted by the rock under it, taller than a whirl of dust and much more slender, turning at 34 degrees a tick against a snow devil's 16, bottom-heavy, and with a smoke core inside the flame. It burns what it crosses (1 to 4 half-hearts off anyone standing in it, 10% to 45% odds of lighting what will burn on a pass) and it now and then spills lava, which is deliberately the rare half of what it does, because lava in open country is a permanent change to it. It dies when it drifts off the molten rock it was standing on, which is a fire whirl over cold ground and therefore a different phenomenon.

The steam devil is the one that is genuinely not weather, and the most interesting of the two because of what it *cannot* do. Lava meeting water flashes it, and the plume organises into a rotating column in seconds, which is why it can appear in the rain, where every other whirl in this mod needs a clear hot afternoon. Steam is hot and it has no mass, so it scalds for 1.5 to 5 half-hearts and moves nothing at all: it has no world pass, no block damage and no debris, and that asymmetry is written as one answer in the model (`movesBlocks`) rather than left to a caller's judgement. It is the whitest thing in the family and the narrowest, it has no dark heart, and it *widens as it rises* - a plume, not a funnel - which is the opposite of every condensation funnel here and the reason it computes its own profile instead of borrowing theirs.

`.freebuff/scratch/Phase3Sim.java` holds all of it - `DerechoModel`, `MonsoonModel`, `LavaWhirlModel` and the two visual profiles - to 275 checks, calling the shipped classes through the source path rather than copies of them. ## Village work: shelter, survey and repair

The villager tornado drill used to walk to the nearest roofed block. That one line is three problems in a village: everyone in the village picks the same doorway, because the nearest roof is the same roof for all of them; a villager walks *toward* the funnel when the close shelter happens to be on its far side; and a village asleep at night never notices the warning at all.

Where to shelter is now arithmetic, in `ShelterChoice`, with no Minecraft types in it, because the decision is the interesting part and it should be measurable without the game. A spot is refused outright when it lies within the funnel's own damage radius of the ground the funnel is *about to cross* - the segment from where the storm is to where its own drift will have carried it a lead time later, not a circle around where it is now, so a shelter the storm has already passed is fine and one in front of it is not. Reaching a spot that means crossing that track costs twenty-five blocks of walking, a spot another villager has already claimed costs twelve, the kinds are ranked bed, door, bell, workstation, plain cover, and there is a small margin penalty for sitting right at the edge of the core, which is survivable but is where the debris is. Out of all that comes one number per spot, and the best one wins. A village with one shelter still shares it; a village with two stops piling into one.

The goal around it reads the world - the funnel's position, drift and half-width once per scan, the roofed places around the villager through the point-of-interest table plus a strided column sweep for doors and plain cover the table cannot name - and then either walks, holds, or runs. Holding matters more than it sounds: the brain's own stroll behaviour is what otherwise walks a villager back out of a house mid-tornado, so while sheltered the walk target is cleared and the position re-asserted. Waking is the other half nobody notices: a warning now wakes sleeping villagers instead of leaving them in bed, which is what they used to do. And when nothing roofed is left - every shelter claimed or in the funnel's path - the villager no longer stands in a doorway the funnel is about to remove, but runs: away from the storm's centre, blended with the perpendicular of its drift, so it leaves the track instead of racing down it.

Two jobs come with it. A **surveyor** villager claims a Survey Station and walks the nearest unsurveyed tornado track in range of its post, rates the damage, and files the rating on the track the survey screen reads - so a village that was hit knows what hit it, and the numbers are the player's own: the reading was extracted from the surveyor item into `DamageScanner` and `DamageScan`, and both callers go through it. A **builder** villager claims a Builders' Bench and repairs storm damage near it, which is exactly the damage a surveyor counts, because both classify through `StormDamage`: scoured ground is re-sodded from whatever the untouched ground beside it is, and a stripped trunk is unbarked back to the block its own name says it came from. A trunk whose original cannot be named is left alone rather than replaced with a guess. The work order is `RepairPolicy`: nearest damage first, sixteen blocks a trip, forty ticks of work per block - the dial that decides whether a village heals over an afternoon or erases the tornado's mark before you turn around. Neither job works in a storm; a funnel overhead takes the villager, and repairing a track while it is still being made is nonsense.

Rebuilding costs the village something, which is what makes it a village rather than a paint job. A repair is paid for out of the builder's own hands, and what a repair costs is the block it is about to put back: an oak log for an unbarked trunk, and for scoured ground the very block the patch is being restored to - so what leaves a chest is exactly what appears in the world, and there is no fuzzy matching to argue with. A builder carrying nothing walks to the nearest chest *within reach that actually holds that block* - a nearer chest of cobblestone is not a source of dirt, and a chest across the village is not a source either - takes up to its carry limit, and works until its hands are empty, at which point it goes back for more. If the village has no such block anywhere it can reach, the job is refused and the damage stands. Nothing about that accounting lives in a mirror of the villager's inventory: the purse is read off the inventory every time it is asked, so what it can afford is what it is actually holding. The whole rule is one config switch, and off is the old behaviour: repairing in place out of nothing.

The accounting itself is `RepairSupplies`, pure and generic over the material key - the goal keys it by item, the harness by string, and both run the same code: what is carried, whether a repair can be paid for, which chest has the goods *and* is in reach, how much to take without overfilling the hands, and which job in the work order is the first one that can be paid for.

Supplies you can watch leave the village. A builder puts the block it is about to place in its arms, where the crossed-arms layer draws it, and that block is drawn smaller as the load runs down: a full trip at its own size, the last block at forty-five per cent of it. A village spending what it hoarded is then something you see rather than something you infer from a chest that is emptier than it was. The size is `SuppliesCarry`, one pure function of what is left and what a trip is worth - monotone by construction, and spent by the same amount at every block, so the shrink is a steady drain rather than a cliff. The tally is read off the villager's own inventory and clamped to a stack, because a count over a stack is a count vanilla refuses to write and an equipment update is read back off a buffer. It reaches the block through a mixin aimed at the single call the layer makes to draw the item: by the time control arrives the pose is already translated into the villager's crossed arms and rotated, so scaling there shrinks the block where it sits and changes nothing else about the villager - and it is scoped to the builder's profession, so every other villager is drawn exactly as vanilla draws it.

A builder that never got round to building was repairing its own village. The lightest scouring and a vanilla village's own street are the same block - `dirt_path` - and so are a scoured trunk and a house's stripped-log corner, so a repair rule that accepts one accepts the other: a builder standing in a village spent its life re-sodding streets and unbarking its neighbours' houses, and the branch that puts up buildings, which only runs with nothing left to repair, was never reached. The same block is a survey's business, because a surveyor counting a road as storm damage is a reading you can explain; a builder bulldozing the village it lives in, endlessly and for nothing, is not. So a repair is now held to the storm's own ground: `TornadoSurveySavedData` keeps a footprint of the tracks filed recently, and a block is damage only if a track covered it - which is what a village's streets and corners are never part of. A builder will re-sod the forms nothing but a storm leaves, `coarse_dirt` and `rooted_dirt`, and it walks past a road. With no track data at all - surveys off, a fresh world - there is no evidence rather than a clean world, and the blocks are trusted, because a builder that repairs nothing is the worse failure.

And a builder refused a repair no longer stops being a builder. A repair the village cannot pay for used to be the end of the attempt, so a village with damage it could not afford sat there with the damage and no house either; the village's own ambition now gets its turn whenever the repairs are done *or* refused. What a builder is doing, and why it is not doing something else, is readable at the bench: right-clicking it runs the builder's own work records, site probe, chest scan and damage sweep, and reports which building is next, what it costs, what is missing, how much damage is left and where the ground it will build on is - from the same helpers the goal itself works from, so it cannot say "ready" while the builder waits on something else.

And a builder with nothing left to repair builds the village a building rather than pretending to. The plan is small and stated: a house first, then the buildings it works out of, then houses again until the village has as many as it is allowed. The middle of that plan is the point of it - a **station building for each job**: after the office comes a survey station for the surveyor, then a workshop for the builder, so a village that has grown a profession ends up owning the job site that profession claims instead of waiting for a player to put one down. All four are piece lists in `VillageBlueprint` - seven by seven, nine by seven, seven by five and seven by seven - and the pieces are checked as geometry: nothing outside its own box, never two pieces in one cell, a foundation that is a ring rather than a slab (the soil inside a house is the soil), walls with no hole in them but the doorway, one door in the middle of the front, three windows at head height, a roof over the whole footprint, and every fixture inside the walls where a villager can reach it. A door is one piece filling two cells and a bed is one piece filling two, which is exactly the difference the chest accounting has to be right about.

Where it goes is arithmetic too, in `BuildSite`: the ground under every column has to be read, has to rise or fall by no more than one block across the whole footprint, has to be dry and solid, and the space the building needs has to be empty. A building is laid on the *lowest* ground under it, so nothing floats and any soil that ends up inside was already there. Sites are tried nearest-first on a fixed grid, which is what makes a half-finished building findable again: the plan is rebuilt from the world every trip and the blocks that are already standing are skipped, so a building interrupted by nightfall, a tornado or a reload is carried on instead of started a second time somewhere else. A site with something in the way that no amount of building will fix is written off and another is looked for.

It costs what it looks like it costs. A material in a plan is a *kind* - planks, logs, stone, glass, a door, a torch, a bed, a chest, the village's own survey station - and the block is chosen from what the chests actually hold, so the same plan builds in oak, spruce or cherry and a village with two stacks of spruce builds in spruce. One piece costs the block it becomes, out of a chest within reach, exactly the way a repair does. And the village has to be able to lay the whole ground layer before it starts a wall, because a foundation half laid and abandoned is worse than no building at all.

What it has built is remembered in `VillageWorksSavedData`, because a village that forgets a house builds it twice - and remembered is not the same as standing: a work is checked by its door cell before it counts, so a village flattened by the next tornado is owed its house again rather than believing it still has one.

The surveyor leaves its rating behind, too. A rating that only lives on the survey screen is a rating nobody standing in the damage will ever read, so the surveyor plants an oak sign where it worked - what it was, how hard it blew, how sure the reading is, and the track's id, on four lines that `SurveyMarker` clips to what a sign actually shows. One sign per track, because the track is marked surveyed when the sign goes up and a surveyor only walks unsurveyed tracks.

Nothing about either job needs a command. A villager takes a profession by claiming a point of interest, so registering a point of interest for each workstation is the whole feature, and the tag that villagers scan (`minecraft:acquirable_job_site`) is where they become findable. Both professions also trade: paper in for the surveyor, who eventually sells the instruments it uses, and the plain blocks a village rebuilds with for the builder.

A village can buy what it is short of, and it pays for it out of its own chests. What it pays is a price table, `VillageExchange`, written down once so that every way of buying from a village agrees with every other one: the counter at the bench, the buy offers its own villagers list, and the builder walking up to whoever is holding the block it needs. Prices are quoted by *kind* of goods - sixteen cobblestone or dirt for an emerald, twenty-four planks, a door and a bed one each - and a partial lot costs a whole one, which is why the bench says "1 emerald per 24" rather than pretending an emerald can be split. The two workstations are the dearest things a village buys and are deliberately priced *below* what its own villagers sell them for, because a village that buys dear and sells cheap is a hole in the economy rather than a shop in it. Selling to one is the half a player takes part in deliberately: hold the material and right-click the bench, and the village buys as much as it is short of, as much as its chests have room for, and as much as its emeralds cover - the money leaves the chest before the goods leave your hand, and a village that can do none of those says so instead of taking anything. Sneaking is left to the game, so the person working on the building the bench stands beside can still build. What a village is short of is not a second opinion either: it is the same shopping list the builder works from, repairs before buildings, which is why the counter's offer and the builder's errands can never disagree about what comes next.

A village's money is not a number in save data. It is the emeralds in its chests, put there by the players who paid its villagers - which is what `VillageTradeIntake` is for. Vanilla deletes a trade's payment: a player sells a stack of cobblestone to a villager and is paid an emerald, and the cobblestone is gone. For a villager with one of this mod's benches in reach, the payment lands in the village instead - emeralds into its chests, and the goods into the hands of the villager who accepted them, who is then a villager the builder can buy that stack back from. That is what makes a village's own traders a place materials come from rather than scenery, and because the payment is read off the offer as the trade completes, nothing is created: a village's spending is bounded by exactly what has been paid to it, and a village nobody trades with cannot buy anything at all.

What finds all of this is one walk, `VillageStore`: the containers a village owns, found by walking columns of ground rather than a container list, because vanilla has no point of interest for a chest and a village keeps its stores indoors as often as in the open. The same walk feeds the builder's pricing, a trade payment's deposit and a bounty's payout - three scans that disagreed about which chests a village owns would be three different villages. The builder's trip is two legs when it has to be: fetch the emeralds out of a chest into its own hands, then walk to the nearest villager actually holding the block, because a villager with none of it is not a seller, and swap - the goods leave the seller's hands only once the emeralds are really in the builder's. A builder that arrives and finds it cannot pay hands the goods back and ends the trip having spent nothing but the walk.

`.freebuff/scratch/VillagerJobsSim.java` measures it in three kinds of claim, in 774 checks, and the third kind is the one that keeps finding things. The arithmetic: the funnel's path is refused and the ground behind it is not, crossing costs exactly the constant it names, a claimed bed still beats plain cover but loses to an unclaimed door, the second villager of a pair really does pick a different shelter, all-refused really is no choice at all, urgency is monotone and full at the core, and the rating ladder is monotone under each indicator with every label boundary checked on the number either side of it. The wiring: that the item and the villager read damage through the same scanner, that a builder repairs only what a surveyor counts - scoped to the method that actually places a block, because a builder that still *finds* trunks while no longer repairing them passes a file-wide search, which is how a break slipped past an earlier version of this check - and that the shelter goal is not quietly back to nearest-roof. That scoping is now load-bearing in a second way: the slice throws if either of its markers has moved, because when the neighbouring method was renamed the slice silently ran to the end of the file and went on passing while testing nothing. The catch test itself had the same disease for a while and was the harder one to notice: it searched the report for "0 failures", and "30 failures" contains that string - so a broken tree with a round number of failures read exactly like a clean one. It is anchored on the count now, and a patch that cannot find its snippet is reported as the script's own failure rather than as an unexplained missed break. The data: that each profession's job site is a point of interest that was actually registered, that both are in the tag villagers scan, and that every block model's textures exist - including the vanilla ones, checked inside the client resource jar - along with the loot, recipe and language entries that make the blocks real. Both overlay folders are checked, not just the living one: a villager that gets bitten and cured still has its profession, and the zombie renderer asks for the same art from a different folder, which is the same bug wearing a green shirt. The 1.12.2 art is the art again, and finding out why it was not is the interesting part. This mod used to ship its regenerated cloud sheets - `cloud256`, `clouds`, `cloud64`, `downfall3`, `rain`, `rain_white`, `debris_1/2/3` and the rest of coroutil's particle set - at Weather2's *own* resource paths, under its own file names. A pack that ships a file at another mod's path wins the resource lookup, so Weather2's originals never reached the particle atlas at all: not for this mod's puffs, and not for Weather2's own deck, scatter funnel or debris either, which were drawing the replacement without anybody choosing it. The two sets are also not close. Measured over one frame each, Weather2's `cloud256` is a single 256x256 sheet, mean alpha 13.2 of 255 with a brightest texel of 81; the regenerated one is 256x2048, eight frames at a frametime of 6, mean alpha 75.4 with a brightest texel of 224. That is a five-fold difference in the body of one puff, and the density of a puff is what decides whether hundreds of stacked billboards sum into cloud or solidify into a faceted slab.

The originals are back by *not shipping them*, which is the part worth keeping: Weather2's jar still carries the 1.12.2 sheets, so the fix is to stop standing in front of them. The regenerated set moved to `coroutil:particles/modern/`, is stitched through this mod's own atlas manifest, and `ParticleArt` is the single place that chooses: `storm_look.originalTextures` (on by default) decides between Weather2's own sheet and this mod's, no spawn site touches the choice, and a sheet missing from the atlas falls back to the other set rather than to the missing-texture checkerboard. Six of those sheets are read straight off Weather2's `ParticleRegistry` by its own code, so the selection is written into those fields too - on the next client tick rather than inside the stitch event, because the tick is unambiguously after every mod's stitch handler has run and the fields are read when a particle is created, not when it is drawn.

Moving the folders also uncovered a real bug in the atlas manifest: `cloud256classic` and `cloud256combined` were never listed as sources of the particles atlas, so the sprites the classic and combined looks asked for resolved to nothing and both quietly fell back to the dense funnel sheet - the exact failure those sheets exist to prevent, wearing the fix's name. All three role sheets are stitched explicitly now.

Our own spawned puffs needed one number moved rather than anything rebuilt: the dust, the debris cloud and the supercell structure alphas were tuned against the regenerated sheet's body, so the density compensation they were already scaled by is now read live off `storm_look.originalTextures` instead of being a constant of 1 - about 5.7 on Weather2's own sheet, clamped to full opacity at every site, so the soft sheet recovers most of the weight it would otherwise lose. The funnel and the deck need nothing: they are Weather2's own puffs, and the faint sheet is the art that population was built against.

## Instruments in the hand, cables in the ground, and a counter that reads them

Every measurement in this mod was already made, and all of it was read standing still at a block: the wind at the anemometer, the column at the barometer, the rain at the gauge, the air from the biome's own number. That is the wrong shape for the one thing a chaser does, which is walk into a field and hold something up. So the same three readings now come in the hand - an anemometer, a thermometer and a barometer - and what each of them is *for* is a model, `HandheldInstrument`, arithmetic only, so the ladders, the scale, the gust share and both instruments' memories are checkable without a client. All three are registered, have their own icons, models and recipes, and are sold at a new counter; one switch turns them off and leaves them inert rather than unregistering them, because a world that already has some should not lose them.

The anemometer is the one worth carrying, and the reason is that it reports the gust and the bearing separately rather than one number, because on a storm's inflow that is the whole question: thirty miles an hour means something entirely different arriving on a gust front than drifting in from a ridge line, so the instrument prints the wind, where it is coming from, and the peak. It also remembers, which is the part a device is for - the gust worth writing down has usually already gone past by the time a chaser arrives - so it keeps the highest reading since it was last cleared, and sneaking clears it, which is the button a real handheld anemometer has. The peak is only printed when it is genuinely above the air right now, since a steady wind and its own peak are the same number and *gusting 46* beside a 46 mph reading is noise, and whether the air is gusty is one share: 0.45 of the reading and above is air that is being stirred rather than blown, which is what a gust front and a funnel's inflow both look like on a dial. The ladder those readings are named on is the block instruments' own, deliberately, so a handheld anemometer and the one on the wall behind it cannot describe the same wind differently.

The thermometer had to invent its scale, and inventing it out loud is the point. Vanilla's biome temperature is not a physical quantity - it is a number the game uses for snow and rain - so the conversion is stated once, in the model: five degrees below freezing at zero and twenty-five degrees per unit above it, which puts plains country at 15 degrees, a taiga at 20 and a desert at 45, a range a player can check against the world they are standing in. Height is the second input, because a thermometer that only knew the biome would call a mountain summit in plains country a mild afternoon; the lapse it uses is the real one, the standard atmosphere's 6.5 degrees per kilometre, rather than the game's own altitude term, which is a fifth of a degree a block and would make every hill its own climate. Every band the readout names comes off that one function, so the words and the number beside them cannot drift apart.

The barometer is the only one of the three that measures something about the future, and it does it out of its own record rather than out of the storm. A rate is only ever a statement about two readings, so the instrument keeps the last one and when it was taken, and the tendency it reports is one this device measured: hold it once and it says it has no record yet, which is the honest answer rather than a number that looks like a measurement, and hold it again after a day of nothing and it says the same, because a rate measured over an interval that long is a rate about a different sky. The memory is kept per player rather than in the item stack, because it is a record of what the holder has seen - dropping the instrument and picking it up again does not rewind the sky, and two barometers in two players' hands are two records - and it is dropped when a player leaves, so a long-lived server does not accumulate one per player who ever held one. Both field instruments refuse to read under a roof: a wind or pressure reading taken inside a room is a reading of the room, and the model says so rather than letting the instrument invent one. The thermometer answers anywhere, because indoor air is still air.

A cable already ran through this mod and it already did one job: a siren arms when a run of wire from it reaches a control panel. A wire does not know what is on its ends, so the same bounded walk is exactly what a sensor network needed, and both questions are now answered by one walk - which is why a sensor network and an armed siren can never disagree about where a cable goes. An instrument within one block of any wire on the run is on the network: attachment is adjacency because the wire block has no facing and draws no connection to the instrument, which is also how a player expects to wire something up. The run is walked from the weather computer, and a run longer than the configured limit counts as no connection at all, so one machine cannot read every instrument in a loaded world; no chunk is ever loaded to look; and an instrument that happens to sit beside two wires of the same run is one instrument, because that is what a player means by wiring a gauge to a cable passing on both sides of it. A network is complete when it carries all three kinds, and what it is missing is reported as the instruments it is missing, because *network partial* is not a diagnosis - and a cable with nothing on it and no cable at all are told apart deliberately, since a run that stops one block short of the instrument is the usual mistake and it has to be able to say so.

What a network buys over one instrument is measured rather than asserted. The model reports the spread of one kind's instruments across the run in blocks, and a reach of one plus that spread against the 160 blocks a single instrument can see, capped at four: three gauges in one garden are a garden, and three gauges across six hundred blocks are a line of storms crossing a county. The weather vane is deliberately not a site on the network, because it reads direction and folding degrees into arithmetic that adds up miles per hour would be nonsense; it is still worth wiring for what it does on its own, which is to be understood from across a base. Reading the network happens when something asks - a player using the computer, or an open screen on its refresh - and never as part of a per-tick world pass.

The computer itself became a place to read rather than a thing that prints. It already knew all of this and delivered it as a column of chat messages, which is a readout rather than an interface: a machine that shows one product and steps to the next on a right-click is a machine you cannot read, because the moment you step past the radar to see the warnings the radar is gone. So the three questions are tabs, in the order they are asked - what the sky is doing over this station now, what it has already done here, and what is in force - and the strip's arithmetic is in `ComputerTab` rather than in a render method: it wraps in both directions, so a click one tab to the left of the first is the last tab and an index saved by an older version reads a valid tab rather than nothing. The scope is drawn from the same echo field the storm map uses, at the station's own origin; the other two tabs are lines gathered by `ComputerFeed` out of things that already exist - the alert ladder's own list and its own colours, so every distinct product in force is shown strongest first rather than only the one a siren would sound, the player's own chase ledger, spotter log and album, and the wire's own walk. Nothing is worked out a second time, so a line that reads wrong reads wrong in the place it came from. The lines travel as a payload capped at twenty-four lines of ninety-six characters, and each payload names the computer it came from, so a screen showing one machine's page cannot be fed another's when a player walks between two of them; sneaking while right-clicking keeps the old chat readout, for anyone who wants the whole thing in their log.

A village has a fourth job, and it is the first one that is not needed to build the other three: a village can house, feed, repair and supply itself without ever looking at the sky, so a meteorologist only exists where a village has a station and a reason to write down what the sky did. A villager takes a profession by claiming a point of interest, so registering the weather station as one - and in the tag villagers scan - is the whole of the mechanism, and the counter trades paper and probe sensor parts in for instruments out, up five rungs to the weather computer itself. The building is the sixth in the plan and the smallest one a village knows how to build: seven by five, a ring of foundation rather than a slab, walls with the doorway in the middle of the front, a roof, and inside it the station block with a chest on either side and a torch. It goes in after the three job sites a village needs in order to keep building - the survey station, the workshop and the timber yard - and before the second house, which is the first point in the plan at which a village spends something on a want rather than a need. A village is paid for it like anything else, out of the published price list: one weather station for ten emeralds, deliberately below the twenty-four its own meteorologist charges for one, because a village that buys cheap and sells dear is a shop and one that does it the other way round is a hole in the economy.

The two defence blocks are fortified for two different reasons, and that is the whole of the feature. Obsidian-reinforced glass is fortified because of what it is made of: a pane of it in the middle of a field is still set with obsidian, and the mod's own ingest, wind and hail passes refuse it exactly as they refuse bedrock. A structural rebar is fortified because of what is holding *it* - it holds the block it stands in, the eight beside it and a column above them - and the reach is a column rather than a sphere on purpose, because the load a vortex puts on a wall is vertical: it is trying to lift the top of it, and a foundation that grips the bottom three blocks is what resists that, where a sphere would anchor a cellar and leave the roof it was put there to hold. A rebar has to be planted: a rod waving in the air or stuck to the side of a wall holds nothing, itself included, and that rule is also what makes one useful, because a rod hanging in a doorway is scenery. The rod itself counts as held even with nothing under it, since a rod lying on the ground being blown away is not a mechanic. Its integration with the rest of the mod is one method, `Fortification.fortified`, asked by the class every hazard here already asks before it edits a block: the wind's pickup and impact passes, the hail, the derecho's roof stripping and the volcanic ingest. So a fortress is a fortress to every storm at once rather than to the one pass a player happened to test, and there is exactly one place to look when somebody says a wall came off anyway. The scan that answers it for a rebar is bounded - a square of columns around the candidate, from one block below it up to the configured reach, ninety positions at the very most - and only ever runs for blocks a damage pass was already considering.

Eight knobs in four new sections: the instruments and how long one takes to settle between readings, which is what stops a held button from filling the chat, since a reading *is* a chat message; the network, and how many wire blocks a run from a computer may cross before it counts as no connection; reinforced glass, rebar anchoring, and how high a rebar reaches; and the meteorologist, whose profession is registered regardless and only loses its trades, so a world that wants the block as decoration can have it. Every one of them is read at a call site the harness finds by walking the tree, because a switch nothing consults is a switch that lies. The three new blocks are craftable, mineable with a pickaxe and drawn by a generator: the station as a painted instrument face, the pane as a fourteen-pixel obsidian frame set with almost-clear glass, a diagonal highlight and four corner fixings - which is what says *reinforced* rather than *dusty* - and the rebar as a weathered steel rod with ribs, pitting and a sawn face at each end.

The thermometer's scale is this mod's own and is stated so that it can be argued with: it will not agree with another mod's thermometer standing in the same air, and it is the one number in the three instruments that is a choice rather than a measurement. The vane is not on the network on purpose, so a base with a vane and nothing else has a cable and no readings, which is the intended reading of that base. And the counter deals in paper, sensor parts and instruments rather than the storm logs and photographs its own description suggests, because the records this mod keeps about a player are saved data, already paid for by the bounty a confirmed report pays and the score a photograph gets: inventing a storm log item to satisfy the wording would have added a second, worse record beside the ones the mod already keeps.

## Hail you can hear land

The mod has drawn hail for a long time and, since the damage pass, let it break things. What it never did was let a player *hear* it - and hail is the one hazard here a player meets first through their ears: you are inside, the light goes green, and then the roof starts. So the stones now land on the things around you, and every surface answers in its own material. A wooden roof rattles, stone ticks, metal rings and goes on ringing, a glass pane pings, a field of grass is a wet patter with no ring in it at all. It is deliberately not looped storm ambience: it is the stones themselves, played where they landed.

What a surface is made of is read off the block's *own* sound type rather than off a second list of blocks this mod would have to keep in step with every new one. `HailClatter.surfaceOf` is handed the resource path of the sound the block itself makes, and sorts it into six ear-materials - soft ground, wood, stone, ice, glass and metal - in a deliberate order: glass before stone, metal before wood (a copper roof is metal even when the block is called a grating), snow as soft ground rather than as ice, because powder snow is not a hard surface and does not tick like one. A name the class has never seen is stone, which is the honest default, because most solid blocks are. Six rather than one sound per block type, because what a player learns from the clatter is whether they are under wood, stone, metal or glass or out in a field - and a seventh file would say something nobody can hear.

How hard a stone lands is the size ladder the damage pass already owns. Measured at the shipped numbers: a pea in a field plays at 0.139 of volume and a baseball on a metal roof at 0.95, nearly seven times as loud, with a pea on wood at 0.247 and a quarter on a glass roof at 0.659. Pitch moves the same way, and on the material as well as the size - the brightest strike in the set is a pea on metal at 1.40 and the deepest a baseball in a field at 0.585, a two-and-a-half-fold spread - and every strike takes a jitter of up to 0.06 of pitch, so a rattle of identical stones is not one sample repeated.

The rate limit is what makes that a hailstorm rather than a machine gun, and it is per surface *and* per player rather than global. Each surface has its own silence between strikes, and the two ringing materials are asked less often than the four that thud, because a ring has not finished before the next strike arrives: a stone's gap is five ticks plus its own rung on the ladder and a ringing surface's is nine plus its rung, so a pea on wood repeats at six ticks, a quarter on stone at nine and a baseball on metal at fifteen - a third of a second at the fastest, three quarters at the slowest, and bounded so that no surface is asked faster than every four ticks or left silent longer than twenty-four while stones are still falling. Because the rhythm is per surface, a player under a glass roof with a metal barn beside them and a field beyond hears all three at once, which is correct: three different things really are being hit. Because it is also per player, two players standing in the same storm do not silence each other, and the record is dropped when a player leaves.

What a player hears where is a per-player scan rather than a state on the storm. The roof over their head is asked first, and it is found by walking up from the player's own head, so someone under three blocks of stone hears stone while someone in the open hears the field under their feet - and the walk stops after six blocks, because a tower does not shelter the ground beside it and the honest answer there is that the stones are landing in the open. Around that, three columns are sampled at random offsets inside the model's own listening radius, which grows from seven blocks for peas to twelve for baseballs, so a player under a wooden roof with a metal barn thirty blocks away hears the wood under them and, when a sample lands on the barn, the metal too. Each strike is played at its own position, so the game's own attenuation does the rest, and it fades to 0.15 of its weight at eighteen blocks and is silent past that: the useful thing the clatter tells a player is where it is. The whole thing runs off the damage pass, with the footprint the damage pass has already worked out, so the clatter starts exactly where the mod says hail is falling and stops when it stops.

Three knobs sit in the existing `hail` section - the switch, the listening radius (twelve blocks by default) and a volume multiplier - and every one of them is read at a call site the harness finds by walking the tree. The radius is a ceiling rather than a second opinion: the model's own size ladder caps it, because a pea's clatter is not worth chasing across a field and a baseball's is. The six sounds are this project's own, synthesised by `.freebuff/scratch/GenHailClatter.java` - resonant partials with a short attack for the materials that ring, crackle grains for ice, a body of noise for the ones that thud, each normalised and closed with a 10 ms fade - and encoded to Ogg Vorbis at 0.16 / 0.22 / 0.18 / 0.20 / 0.26 / 0.42 seconds. All six are registered with a subtitle in both the sounds file and the language file, so a deaf player reads what they cannot hear.

`.freebuff/scratch/Phase5Sim.java` holds it to all of that in 451 checks, and they are mostly arithmetic rather than text: the material table surface by surface and by precedence, a nameless sound path refused, the volume ladder at both ends with the metal roof's own figure, the pitch of every material inside the game's accepted range, the jitter's span and the roll clamped rather than trusted, the pace of a thudding and a ringing surface with both bounds, the listening radius growing with the ladder, the roof looked for six blocks overhead, the fade measured against the distance it is heard across - the faintest share falling exactly where that distance ends and half a block past it silent - and the budget on how many strikes one pass may produce. Then the wiring, as text, because it cannot be computed: that the damage pass calls the clatter pass last, that a leaving player's rhythm is forgotten, that each of the six surfaces has a registered sound, a subtitle and a translation, that every knob's define name is the name the language file explains it under, and that the six files in the tree really are Vorbis audio of the length they claim to be.

Sixty-five deliberate breaks are each caught, and the tree is restored byte-identical afterwards. They move a material's own label, sound name, loudness or tone; sort glass after stone and metal after wood; read snow as ice; make an unknown name soft; flatten the size ladder; carry the volume past full; invert the pitch drop and widen the jitter; remove the roll's clamp; swap the two spacings and move both bounds; stop the listening radius growing with the stones; look for a roof at the wrong height; cut the heard-across distance and drop the faintest share to nothing; let one pass fire a wall of strikes; key the pacing by nobody in particular; play a material off the wrong sound and glass off the ice sound; take the listening distance from the knob alone; remove the clatter from the hail pass and run it before the stones land; keep a leaving player's rhythm forever; unregister one of the six sounds and register another under a name nothing can load; rename a knob away from its translation and leave a knob nothing reads; lose a subtitle from the sounds file and from the language file; and replace each of the six audio files in turn with another material's sound, cut one down to nothing and turn another into something that is not the container the game reads.

The first full run of this sweep caught 53 and missed 6, and the misses were worth more than the catches. Two of the six breaks broke nothing a measurement could see: one removed the outer clamp in `pitchFor`, which cannot fire because the ladder is inside the game's accepted range by construction - the model now says so where the clamp used to be, and that break is a material's tone moved out of range instead - and one removed the clamp on the dice roll, which no check had ever probed with a roll outside nought to one, so nothing noticed it was load-bearing. The other four moved a constant that the checks were reading back out of the model rather than asserting, so moving the constant satisfied them: the heard-across distance, the faintest share, the pass interval and a subtitle. None of the four was fixed by loosening a check. The constants are pinned in the harness now - the pace, the roof height, the two bounds on it, the heard-across distance and the share it fades to, the pass interval, and the subtitle key matched in full rather than by prefix - and the four are caught.

Four honest notes. I cannot play this, so whether six-millimetre stones *read* as hail rather than as gravel is a thing a session has to settle; every number above is the model's arithmetic and the audio files' own measured durations rather than something I heard. The material is a judgement the game has already made about each block, not a list this mod keeps, which is what lets a roof of any new block sound like what it is made of - and also means a block whose sound type is unhelpful will be heard as stone. The clatter is six sounds and not one per material, so a lead roof and a copper one sound the same; they are both metal to an ear, and the alternative was a file per block. And the radius knob is a ceiling rather than the whole answer, which is measured in the harness rather than promised: the model caps it by the size of the stones, so at the shipped twelve a pea is only listened for out to seven blocks.

## The sky over a hailing cell

A storm's darkening used to be the game's. `ClientWeatherEffects` wrote a smoothed storm severity into the level's rain level, and vanilla derived the sky's colour, the sun's drawn alpha and the world's ambient light from it. That worked, and it had a ceiling nobody chose: vanilla's rain darkening is its own rain curve, so the darkest a storm could ever make the sky was wherever that curve ended. Measured at the shipped scale, against the game's own arithmetic transcribed from `ClientLevel.getSkyDarken` rather than remembered, the old path stopped at **0.631** - and with 1 as a clear noon and 0.200 as vanilla's own midnight, a storm could never take the sky past a cloudy day. That is the whole reason for the change, and the same measurement is what it lands on now: the mod's deck on its own takes the sky to **0.473**, a direct hit with a hailing core to **0.320**, and a scale of 1.0 to vanilla's own **0.200**.

The darkening is one reading applied in four places: the sky's colour, the fog's, the cloud deck's, and the world's own ambient light through `getSkyDarken`, which is the single value the lightmap is built from - plus the sun, the moon and the stars through the one alpha vanilla draws all three with, `1 - rainLevel`. Nothing is written into the level to get any of it, and the one write that is left is named for what it is: the shader-pack fallback pushes a rain level so that vanilla draws its own rain sheets when this mod's rain program cannot run, which is about precipitation and not about light. Two readings that used to arrive through that pushed value now ask for it by name instead - the mist's own rain air and the morning's weather moisture both take the greater of the game's rain and the mod's storm - so a storm still fogs the air and still dampens a dawn.

The green is the part worth having. A green sky is light coming through a storm's hail core, and this mod already knew exactly where its hail was, so the sky is read off the hail's own model rather than off a second opinion about the weather: `HailModel.hasHail`, its size ladder, and the same `StructureSample` footprint the damage pass and the clatter both run on, over storms within 700 blocks. Nothing below a quarter-sized stone turns any sky green, however hard it is raining - which is the gate that keeps a green sky meaning hail - and above it the green rises with the stone and with the depth: a quarter in the middle of its shaft is a third of the way there, a golf ball two thirds, a baseball all of it, with a tenth of the footprint as dead band at the rim before any green shows at all. The deepest hailing core is what darkens the sky over the player and the best-greening one is what greens it, and those need not be the same storm, because a hail core two hundred blocks away is a green sky over there whatever is falling on you.

The colour is a multiply rather than a blend toward grey, which is the one place this deliberately parts company with vanilla's rain sky. Vanilla mixes the sky toward its own luminance, which is what turns rain a flat blue-grey whatever the hour; multiplying by the darkness keeps the hue the day gave it, so a storm at sunset is a dim orange and a storm at noon is dim white. The green goes on last, as a mix toward a muted yellow-green (0.35, 0.60, 0.38), and the tint is itself scaled by the darkening - so at a third dark the mix lands on a dark green, and there is no input that produces a bright green sky. The deck takes 55% of the green and the fog 35%, because the deck is the surface the light is coming through and the air in front of you is not the thing that turned green. Nothing here reads the clock: it all multiplies a colour that already carries the sun's angle on the day, so a green at midnight comes out a green so dark it is barely there, which is right and falls out of the arithmetic instead of being a condition somebody had to remember.

The smoothing is two rates in each direction, per sky update (every four ticks by default): the deck rises at 0.30 and falls at 0.12, the green at 0.28 and 0.10. Two rates because the two directions are different events - a deck arriving is a storm arriving, and a deck leaving is weather that has already gone - so a single rate would either snap the sky clear the moment the last stones passed or fade a storm in out of nowhere. The green falls more slowly than the deck on purpose: a sky that has been green stays in the eye for a moment after the core has moved off it.

Four knobs, in the `visuals` section that already held the old two: `visuals.stormSkyDarkening` and `visuals.stormSkyDarkenScale` now mean the mod's own darkening and how far into it a storm goes (0.85 by default, which leaves the floor somewhere to go), and `visuals.hailSkyGreen` with `visuals.hailSkyGreenStrength` (0.85) are the green and how much of it. Every one of them is read at a call site the harness finds by walking the tree, and the master visuals switch stands the whole reading down rather than leaving a half-remembered sky behind.

`.freebuff/scratch/Phase6Sim.java` measures all of it in 168 checks. The arithmetic is the model called straight out of the shipped sources: the darkening at both ends and at its floor, monotone across the whole grid of both of its inputs, a scale of nought taking no light and one past that clamped, half a deck over half a shaft landing on exactly half the depth, and the comparison against the old path above. The green is checked gate by gate - no stones, a pea, a marble, a dime, a quarter, a golf ball, a baseball - on the depth either side of the tenth-of-a-footprint dead band, and for monotonicity as a core comes over. The colour is checked for the property vanilla's own rain sky does not have (half the light is half the colour, with the hue preserved exactly), for the fully-green case landing exactly on the tint at that darkness, and for no channel ever being brighter than the darker of the sky it came from and the tint. Then the sun and the light as floors, the smoothing at both of its rates in both directions, the shaft depth at the middle, the rim and outside it, and the wiring as text: the same four hail functions, the mixins that apply the reading, the registration order that keeps a whiteout winning over the sky a player is standing under, the two consumers that used to read the pushed value, every knob's name matching the words for it, and that the model imports nothing from the game at all.

Eighty-two deliberate breaks are each caught, and the tree is restored byte-identical afterwards. They move every constant the model has - the floor, the two shares of the darkening, the size gate, the dead band at the rim, each tint channel, both green shares, all four smoothing rates, the hail scan range - and each of its functions: the hailing core dropped out of the darkening, the strength added rather than multiplied, the cover and the depth read straight off their callers, a reading that is not a number taken as darkness, the ladder flattened and then stretched and then started a rung too low, the depth share no longer normalised, the darkening turned into a brightening, the tint no longer darkened, the sun hidden in proportion to the light instead of the dark, the world lit by the darker of two readings, the shaft depth inverted so the rim is the middle, both one-rate and no-rate smoothing, a target and a reading past the end of the scale, a footprint of no size taken as the middle of one. Then the wiring: every storm read as one with hail in it, the stones sized off something other than the storm's own character, an unguarded snapshot trusted for its shaft, the nearest hailing core winning over the deepest, the scan and the green and the darkening each read out whatever their switches say, the shader fallback pushing rain whatever the shader state, the sky read from a deck of nothing, the mist and the morning no longer asking for the storm's own rain, the world's light and the sky's colour and the deck's left to the game, the sun left to blaze through a storm, the number taken off the wrong call in the wrong method and the injection no longer required, the storm's fog never registered and then registered after the tornado's, the green shipped off and its strength shipped at full, the darkening shipped halfway, a knob renamed away from the words for it, the green's name removed from the language file and then its explanation, and the darkening's scale left without one.

The first run of this sweep caught 80 of 82, and both misses were worth more than the catches. One was a weak check of the harness's own: it compared the world's light against a game reading of 1.5 above this mod's own, where the `min` already wins - which is a true statement about that clamp, and the honest reading of it is that the upper end of the clamp is redundant given the `min` and only the lower end is load-bearing, so the check now uses a negative reading, where removing the clamp would light the world with less than nothing. The other miss was simply a check that got dropped while the wiring checks were being rewritten in the same edit, which left the green's own accessor - the place the two switches meet - asserted nowhere at all; it is pinned now. Neither was fixed by loosening a check.

Three honest notes. I cannot play this, so whether a green sky reads as hail rather than as a bug in the renderer is a session's verdict; every number above is the model's arithmetic and a transcription of vanilla's own sky-darken curve rather than something I looked at. The sun's hiding is the same number it always was at the same scale - at 0.85 the sun is drawn with fifteen per cent of its alpha and at 1.0 with none of it - so what changed there is that the number is the mod's own and that raising the scale does exactly what it says, while what changed everywhere else is that the world goes as dark as the sky does. And everywhere the storm's sky meets the game's weather it is a floor: the sun, the moon, the stars and the world's ambient light all take the darker of the two, so a night storm is not twice as dark as a night and an actual thunderstorm settles the question itself. What this deliberately does not do is tell the level it is raining, which is why the two readings that were built on the value it used to write now ask for the mod's own.

## A readout you can read, and a map that stays a map

A forecast display prints six lines - the outlook, the mode, the tornado and wind probabilities, the storms in the scope - and until now it printed them *flat on the plate*, in the plate's own horizontal plane. A plate is a map and a map belongs flat: an outlook's areas, a warning's shape and a storm's position are all read by looking down at them. Text does not work that way, and two things were wrong at once. A line lying in the horizontal plane is foreshortened by exactly the angle it is read from, so the same six lines were legible from directly overhead and a smear from anywhere a player actually stands. And at the scale they were set, nine font pixels at 0.005, a line was about four and a half centimetres tall on a plate three blocks across: the size of a fingernail on a table. Standing the text up and turning it to face the player is the whole change.

`ForecastPanel` is the model behind it, and it holds no Minecraft types at all: the renderer measures the font, hands in pixel widths and distances, and does nothing with the numbers but put vertices where they say. The panel is sized from its widest line, between a floor of 112 font pixels and the 190 that the flat readout was fitted to, and its height is the header band, the body rows and one bottom margin. Its scale grows by a tenth per block past two blocks and stops at two and a fifth times the near scale, so it holds roughly the same size on screen out to about fourteen blocks and *then* shrinks away: legible from where a player stands to read it, a coloured shape with a title from where they only glance at it. Its facing is the yaw of the camera's own offset - `atan2(dx, dz)`, degrees anticlockwise from +Z - and never the camera's pitch, which is what keeps it upright whatever height the player is at, and is the one place this deliberately parts company with a name tag, which lies down as you look at it from above.

The composition was measured rather than guessed, because the sign of it is exactly the kind of thing that looks right in code and faces the back in the world: a probe against vanilla's own camera frame established that `R_Y(faceYaw) * R_X(-18°)` is the screen frame with the world's vertical kept, and that the lean tilts the panel's normal eighteen degrees up in world space from *any* camera position rather than only from level. The lean is why the panel's top is placed and not its bottom: local down runs into the world at the cosine of the lean, so a leaning panel's top is lower above the plate than a vertical one's would be, and `topLiftBlocks` accounts for it exactly - the bottom lands 0.62 blocks up, which is above the plate's own radar icons at about 0.46, so the panel is not drawn through them. It also stands 0.85 blocks back from the plate's middle, because the plate is a map and the middle of it is where the storm being tracked usually is: the player reads the map with the readout behind it rather than through it.

The header band carries the outlook's own category colour, and its label's ink is chosen by luminance at 150 out of 255. That is a compromise and the model says so: the outlook's ladder runs from a pale general-thunder green through a deep marginal green to the high-risk magenta, so no single ink is right on all eight categories, and a label that vanishes into its own band on two of eight is worse than one that is only right on six. The same colour is what the plate's own accent falls back to for a display with no outlook - a track list, a live scan - which is why the accent bands a display too.

The other half of the change is the plate rather than the panel, and it is the honest answer to a smaller complaint: an outlook's fill used to be solid enough to hide what was under it, which is what nesting wants and also what turned the whole sheet, and the ground around the block it stands on, into the outlook's own green. The fill now ships at 0.30 instead of 0.78, and the area's outline earns back what the wash gives up - alpha from 210 to 252 and width by 45% - so the nesting is read from the edges rather than from stacked translucency. Both trades are model methods, `edgeAlpha` and `edgeWidth`, rather than two literals in a renderer. Four knobs: `forecast_readout.readoutPanel` (on), `forecast_readout.readoutScale` (1.0, held to the model's own 0.5 to 2.0), and labels for the four `forecast_polygons` knobs that had none, including the fill's comment rewritten to describe what now ships.

`.freebuff/scratch/Phase7Sim.java` holds it to all of that in 168 checks. Most are arithmetic out of the shipped sources: the panel's metrics and the ways a line count can and cannot map onto them, the width floor and cap and the fit inside them, the last line landing inside the panel's own bottom edge, the scale ladder at both ends and every block in between for monotonicity, the facing in all four quadrants with a full turn swept for range and the same direction at ten times the distance, the standing-back in both axes measured as a distance, the ink boundary either side and the green's own weighting, and the edge trade monotone across the whole opacity range. Then the wiring as text, because it cannot be computed: that all four readouts go through the panel, that the flat readout survives in exactly one place - the fallback behind its switch - that the panel turns on the world's up axis and leans with the sign that tilts its top away, that the font's y-down flip is applied before the pose its quads are written in, and the one ordering that is a crash rather than a blemish: every quad written before the first text call, because asking the buffer source for a font's render type ends the batch the panel's vertices came from. Finally every knob is a knob something reads and a player can read about: the harness looks the translation's *value* up and requires words in it rather than a quoted name being somewhere in the file.

Sixty-four deliberate breaks are each caught, and the tree is restored byte-identical afterwards. They move the leading, the band, the gap, the margin, the header's row, the width floor and cap, the line ceiling and the two clamps that hold a line count; the near scale, the near distance, the growth and its ceiling, the hold's own floor, both bounds on the player's multiplier and the clamp that applies them; the lean, the bottom lift, the standing-back, the cosine the lift accounts for, the facing's two arguments and its wrap, and both signs of the standing-back; the ink threshold, the two inks' order and opacity and the green's weight; the edge's two alphas, its width gain and its two clamps. Then the wiring: a Minecraft type imported into the model, the lean's sign flipped, the panel never turned to face the player, the font's y flip dropped, the panel sized from its cap rather than its widest line, the size knob left unread, the flat readout's fallback emptied, the band drawn in the plate's accent, a border strip missing, the quads moved past the first text call, the plate's own edge back at a fixed alpha and then at a fixed width, the hazard's and the hatch's own pairs bypassed, the outlook's fill back at the solid wash with its comment arguing for it, the panel shipped off, the size knob's key renamed away from its translation and its bounds no longer the model's, and five translations emptied one at a time.

Three honest notes. I cannot play this, so whether a standing panel reads as an instrument board rather than as a hologram left over the block is a session's verdict; every number above is the model's arithmetic rather than something I looked at. The panel deliberately does not hold a constant angular size past fourteen blocks - a readout that did would be five blocks across at ten, which is a billboard and not a readout - so a player who wants it legible from the far side of a village has the scale knob rather than a promise. And the flat readout is not deleted, it is one switch away: this is a change of opinion about how to read a plate, not a claim that the plate was wrong.

## The art a tornado is drawn with

A storm draws two puffs now, and both are the author's own: the cloud that the deck, the structure particles and the loose dust are drawn with, and a darker, sparser one that the funnel's two sheets are drawn with. Neither is generated, resampled or reshaded anywhere in this repository - `GenStormArt` copies each file into the roles that draw it, byte for byte, and the harness compares the installed sheets against those masters with the same comparison it would use on any other file. What the two are, measured rather than described, because every claim below is about these numbers: the cloud is a single 256x256 frame with a frame mean of 0.683 of 255, a peak of 255, 59% of the frame above 200, an opaque centre, a third of full alpha still there beyond 0.94 of its radius, a neutral grey of 127 127 127, and corners that carry almost nothing; the funnel's own puff is the same size and frame count with a mean of 0.466, a peak of 255, 43% of the frame above 200, 59% of its square covered by anything at all, empty corners, and a mid grey of 103 103 103. That pair is the whole art decision, and it is a pair rather than one file because a storm needs two different things from a puff.

The second one exists because a tornado is the one part of a storm that has to read as solid and dark, and no amount of alpha can do that to a sheet whose brightest texel is 81 of 255 - the ceiling is the art. Weather2's own `cloud256` is exactly that: 256x256, one still frame, a frame mean of 0.052 (13 of 255), and a centre alpha of 0.123. It is faint by construction, and faint for a good reason: hundreds of 30 to 37 block deck billboards stacked over each other only sum into one overcast if each of them is that soft. A layered funnel is not that geometry. It is fewer, larger surfaces closer to the camera, and the same art a deck sums into a cloud a funnel sums into a haze - which is why a *wider* funnel read as more transparent rather than less. So the funnel's two sheets are drawn with its own puff instead, and that sheet is deliberately not governed by the art switch: `storm_look.originalTextures` decides the deck, the rain, the debris and the dust, and a tornado draws its own art either way, with a fallback to the selected puff sheet rather than to nothing so that a missing sprite cannot become a checkerboard on the tornado itself. The pair is also not a shade apart: the funnel's puff is 103 grey where the cloud's is 127, so the two are two files rather than one file that could have served both, which is what the harness asserts about them. The honest half of this is the thing no measurement settles, and it splits in two directions. In tone the funnel's puff is the darker of the two, which is the direction a column wants; in body it is sparser across its own square and it ends more sharply: a 0.466 mean against the cloud's 0.683, 59% of its square covered against 94%, a 68-level edge against the cloud's 28, and far less alpha left at its outer ring - 0.055 against 0.330. The funnel's alpha allowance is at full and can only ever hold density back, so a column built from this art is drawn at the puff's own density, and whether that reads as condensation or as smoke is a session's verdict - the numbers are written down here rather than hidden, because this is the author's choice of puff and the harness's job is to record it rather than to argue with it.

The animation this section used to be about is retired, and deliberately rather than by neglect. Both roles' sheets shipped as eight-frame strips with four phase siblings each - the same boil cut with its frame order rolled a quarter of a cycle apart - because the game animates a texture by uploading every frame into the *same* atlas region while a sprite's UVs never move, so a whole storm on one sheet swings between the same eight poses at the same instant and reads as one large texture flipping instead of as cloud turning over. An authored still has no frames to stagger, so the three siblings per role, their atlas entries and their `.mcmeta` clocks are gone, and `storm_look.billboardPhases` ships off with them: every phase resolves to its own role's sheet through the provider's own fallback. The model is kept rather than deleted, and kept whole - `StormBillboardPhase` still hashes a key to a phase, its four offsets are still a quarter of the cycle apart, and the harness still requires both - because that is the way back, and it was the only thing that ever made a storm stop breathing in lockstep. What is not kept is a claim: a sibling file that nothing draws is a file in the atlas and in the download for nothing, and a switch that ships on against four stills is a hash paid for no reason.

## The foot of a small vortex, and the ground under it

The report was that the suction vortices go underground, and the shape of it is worth stating precisely, because both causes are geometry rather than tuning. Every puff of one of these funnels is a camera-facing quad *centred* on the ring it belongs to: `EntityRotFX.setScale(s)` writes `s` straight into `quadSize`, `getQuadSize` returns that field unchanged, and the renderer builds the quad from corners at (±1, ±1, 0) multiplied by it - so a puff drawn at scale 4 reaches four blocks above its ring and four blocks below it, and half a puff's drawn height *is* its width. A sub-vortex's puffs are sized to the ring they sit on, so its foot hangs below that ring by about the width of the ring, which on flat ground is between half a block and three and a half blocks of the foot drawn inside the terrain - least on the small, dense rings and most on a ring that is still forming, because a forming ring carries half the puffs and each of them is drawn wider to cover its share of the circle. The second cause is the height the ring is drawn at. The anchor is the ground under the *centre* column of the vortex - the surface scan behind `clampBaseToGround`, plus 0.42 of a block - and a ring is flat, so on any rise the whole uphill half of the foot, and of the dust skirt below it, which is drawn wider still, is inside the hill. Measured on the shipped silhouette at the renderer's own default width, with the base ring 1.42 blocks above the ground: a 2.2-block foot clears the surface by 0.87; a 6.0-block one by 0.51; a 10.5-block foot that is still forming is **1.71 blocks under it**; a 15.0-block formed foot is 0.95 under; a 15.0-block forming foot is **3.23 under**. And on a rise of one in three, an 11.2-block foot has **3.46 blocks** of its uphill side inside the hill. That is why the complaint named the small vortices: the flatter and the wider the thing the eye is looking at, the more of it is in the ground.

The fix is `FunnelFootHug`, and it is arithmetic on numbers with no game types in it, so it can be measured rather than eyeballed. Each layer is lifted so the lowest point of the puff it is about to place lands on the ground *under that layer*: the highest ground sampled around the circle that layer's puffs are drawn on, plus the puff's half width, plus a clearance of 0.15 of a block. The **highest** bearing rather than the average is the point of it, because a flat ring on a rise is buried on its uphill side, and that is the side a mean would average away. An unmeasured foot is left exactly where the silhouette put it rather than guessed at - a foot that has not been sampled wants no lift, not sea level, which is a distinction a NaN carries and a default does not. Rising is immediate and falling eases a quarter of the way a tick, so a foot is never left buried waiting to catch up and never drops by the height of the bank it just stepped off. And the whole thing is capped at eight blocks, because one bad sample - a cliff, a cave mouth, a chunk edge - must not be able to launch a vortex into the air: past that the silhouette wins and the terrain is allowed to cut the foot.

The ground under a foot is read bearing by bearing: eight bearings around each of the foot's own two circles, the condensation's lowest ring and the wider dust skirt, at two bearings a funnel per tick, so each circle is refreshed every four ticks and the cost is two surface reads a funnel per tick - one heightmap lookup each, against a funnel that already spends up to 26 puffs on each of a couple of dozen rings. A breakdown collar wraps the parent's wall rather than standing on the ground, so both of its circles are handed in as NaN and it samples nothing; a circle narrower than a twentieth of a block is skipped rather than sampled; and a base that jumps more than 64 blocks - a storm re-seeded somewhere else - forgets what it measured at the old one. What is sampled is the funnel's own base, not the storm's position and not the ground centre: the foot is the thing that has to be on the ground, so the foot is the thing measured.

The lift is applied where a puff is placed and nowhere else. A layer's own position stays the track the silhouette asks for, and the placement takes the lifted height as an argument - `layer.pos.y + layer.cloudLift` for the condensation, `layer.pos.y + layer.debrisLift` for the dust - which is also why the ring width and the ring alpha were pulled out into `funnelCloudScale` and `funnelCloudAlpha`: the hug has to ask for the number the puff is actually drawn at, and a second copy of that expression would be a hug tuned against a width nobody draws. The condensation and the dust are lifted by their own half widths - as wide as their rings, and 1.15 blocks respectively - so one shared lift would either leave the cloud buried or float the dust above the soil it is lifting; a ring with no condensation on it asks for no cloud width at all; and the horizontal vortex, which is a tube that wraps the funnel aloft, is not hugged anywhere. All three of the ground funnels do it: the satellite, whose foot circle is its own lowest ring rather than its widest; the sub-vortex, whose two circles come off its own foot radius; and the pre-touchdown genesis whirl, whose foot is the dust column it is drawn as. The result is deliberately confined to the foot - the tallest ring a five-block puff can still bury is 3.7 blocks above the base, so everything above that keeps exactly the shape the silhouette gives it.

`.freebuff/scratch/FootHugSim.java` holds it to all of that in 67 checks, and it calls the model's own methods rather than copies of them, so the mapping from a puff's scale to the distance it reaches below its ring is stated once and checked once. The burial above is measured in the harness rather than asserted, along with the lift that removes it, and the harness also requires that the artifact was really there: each of the five shipped sizes is checked for standing on the ground afterwards, for staying inside the rail while doing it, and - where the foot was genuinely buried - for having needed a lift at all, because a check that a foot is now clear passes just as well when it was never buried. Thirty-six deliberate breaks are each caught and the tree is restored byte-identical afterwards: the half width halved, the mapping from scale to drawn height changed under it, the clearance removed from the height a ring is asked for, the ceiling raised past anything a cliff could ask for and lowered to where it clips the foot instead, nothing sampled a tick, four bearings instead of eight, the highest bearing replaced by the first and by the mean and by an unsampled one read as ground level, rising eased and falling taken at once, the fall rate halved, a jump to a new place that keeps the ground it measured at the old one, a collar given a foot circle, the dust skirt sampled on the condensation's own circle, the ground read where the storm is rather than under the foot, a ring with no condensation given a cloud's half width, one shared ground for the dust and the condensation, both rings placed on the layer's track instead of on their lifts, the ring width typed out at a call site, the dust drawn at the cloud's scale, the whirl's foot read off a funnel wall, the sampling budget typed into the loop, and the horizontal tube put on the ground as well. It is in the standing set now, where it is the one harness that needs the game's own classes as well as this mod's - the silhouette it reads clamps with Minecraft's `Mth` - so `runstand.sh` compiles and runs it against the dev classpath the client launches with; all twenty-eight harnesses are clean, `ArtSim` at 204 checks among them, `GroundChunkSim` at 42, which is the harness the debris section below is about, and `OceanWaveSim` at 73, which is the harness the sea section below is about.

Three honest notes. I cannot play this, and the screenshot that came with the report has no ground in it at all - its lower two fifths are a flat fogged field with no variation anywhere in it - so the numbers above are the ring geometry computed the way the renderer computes it rather than a picture of a foot in a hillside: whether a lifted foot reads as standing on the ground rather than hovering above it is a session's verdict, and that is what the 0.15-block clearance is for. The sweep found two things about itself. `SAMPLES_PER_TICK` and `BEARINGS` are `public static final`, so javac inlines them where they are read, and a harness compiled once before a sweep keeps comparing the shipped figures - two breaks that only move a declared number therefore read as ones nothing catches, and the sweep recompiles the harness against the patched class for every break now. And one break did nothing at all: it replaced "unknown ground" with a value *below* the ring, so the lift still came out at zero. `Break.java` refuses a snippet it cannot find, which cannot help there, because a substitution that applies and changes no answer is a different failure from one that never happens; the only thing that catches it is running the sweep and reading the misses. Which is the reason the constants are deliberately not in the config: the ceiling, the clearance and the sampling budget are the shape of this fix rather than a preference, and a knob for the rail would be a knob for the bug the rail exists to prevent.

## The drone, and the item that is the link

A weather drone is an airframe you fly from where you stand: the item launches it two blocks ahead, the camera moves to it, the movement keys fly it, and everything it flies through - peak wind, lowest pressure, time in the wind, closest approach to a storm - comes home as a flight log. Its flight model has a harness of its own and is not what this section is about. This is about the half of the drone a player meets before any of that: the link between the pilot and the airframe, which is held by the pilot's hand and by nothing else.

The rule is written in one place, in the airframe: while the pilot is no longer holding a drone item, the link is dropped on that tick and the drone holds station on its own. That is a good rule - a pilot who has put the transmitter away has stopped flying - but the item spent itself on the launch, and the spend was guarded by `!player.getAbilities().instabuild`. That guard is the whole shape of the bug. Launching a drone consumed the only drone in the hand for a survival player and left it there for a creative one, and since the airframe drops its pilot the moment that hand is empty, a survival player was let go one tick after deploying it - camera back on their own head, the drone holding station out of reach, the link gone before they touched a key - while the same item flew perfectly in creative, which is where it had been tested. A survival-only path guarded by an instabuild test is a path that only fails in the one game mode nobody tests in.

The fix is that rule read from the other end. The item stays in the hand in every game mode, because it is not a consumable: it is the transmitter, and one item in the hand is one drone. Nothing is spent by a launch and nothing is minted by a recall - the drone comes home onto the item that was flying it and its flight log is written onto that stack, where the old recall built a fresh stack and put it in the inventory, which added an item for a creative player and dropped the recovered drone on the ground when the inventory was full. The hand rule is one rule now rather than two, either hand carrying the link, which is how the airframe has always read it; the client read the main hand only, so a pilot flying with the drone offhand was reported as having let go, a mismatch that healed itself on the next tick and therefore never read as more than a flicker.

What deliberately did not change is what happens when the wind wins. A drone destroyed by a tornado still drops its wreck where it fell with the log it had reached on it, because the log is the measurement and a drone that leaves nothing is a measurement nobody can read. The wreck is a drone item, so a retrieved wreck is a spare airframe; against a recipe of four iron, four glass panes and a redstone that is salvage rather than an exploit. And a recall still needs the drone inside `droneLinkRangeBlocks`, so a drone flown past its range is a walk and not a button.

There was a second version of the same complaint at the other end of the flight, and it was found by reading the two numbers side by side rather than by flying. The airframe was sent to its pilot's own client for 16 chunks - 256 blocks - while the link it ships with reaches 320, so a drone flown past 256 blocks came off the camera and out of the pilot's ears while the server still had a live link and the airframe was still answering the keys: the same symptom as the launch bug, at a range that the drone's own configuration encourages a player to fly to. It is tracked to 32 chunks now, 512 blocks, and the harness asserts the covering rather than the number, because the pair can drift apart from either side - `droneLinkRangeBlocks` is a knob whose default is nearly a fifth further than the tracking was.

`.freebuff/scratch/DroneFlightSim.java` already existed and holds all of this - 54 checks now, up from 47 - but it had never been in the standing set, and the reason is worth writing down: it printed `all drone flight checks passed` rather than a count, `runstand.sh` reads a verdict as a pass or as a failure count, and a harness that answers in neither is reported as `NO VERDICT` and then ignored. The drone's own 47 checks had therefore been sitting outside the regression sweep since the day they were written, which is the quietest way for a harness to stop being evidence. It answers in the same count as every other harness now and is in the list. The group this change added reads the launch and the recall as slices of `use()` rather than as searches of the file, because both halves live in that one method: a file-wide "the item is never spent" passes while the recall mints a stack, and a file-wide "the log goes onto the hand" passes while the launch eats the item - which is this bug, one game mode deep.

Fifteen deliberate breaks are each caught, and the tree is restored byte-identical afterwards. Six of them are the link rule: the item spent on launch again for anyone without instabuild, an airframe that keeps flying for an empty hand, a recall that mints a stack, a log written onto the wrong hand, a client reading the main hand only, and a wreck that carries an empty log. The rest are the load-bearing claims elsewhere in the harness - an airframe sent to its pilot for 256 of the 320 blocks its link reaches, a link range of sixteen blocks, the range no longer dropping the link, a control payload that answers whoever is nearest rather than its own pilot, a movement freeze the engine is never told about, a camera mixin that is not applied, a rotor sound with no asset behind it, and the integrator back on the force-and-drag model whose steady state sat 17% above the configured airspeed. One of them was missed on the first run and the fault was the harness's own: the camera mixin check asked whether the name `CameraDroneMixin` appeared anywhere in the loader's file, so a mixin misspelled to begin the same way passed. It reads the client list and the quoted name now, which is the difference between asserting that a mixin is applied on the client and asserting that sixteen letters are somewhere in a document.

Two honest notes. I cannot play this, so whether the link holds through a real session is a session's verdict - but the mechanism is not a guess: the link is server state tied to the hand, so the fix is the same statement the flight model already makes, and it is checked on both sides at once. And while this was being fixed, `weatherDrone` turned out to be a switch that does nothing at all: it is declared, commented, and read nowhere in the mod, so turning the drone off in the config changes nothing. That is a real bug of the quieter kind and it is not fixed here, because gating the item without the airframe would leave a half-off drone; it wants its own pass.


## Debris: what a storm throws, and what it is made of

The debris a tornado throws used to be one of two things, and neither of them was the ground: a vanilla stick item, or a Weather2 twig sprite whose colour was mixed from how dry the ground under it was - a table of twenty-eight blocks plus the water in the same column, from sand at 1.0 and five terracottas at 0.9 down through gravel, five dirts and six stones to grass and moss at 0.0, with snow, ice, mud and open water at 0.0 and everything else at 0.10. So a debris field could only say where you were standing by being tinted the local dust colour, and a scoured field, a desert and a gravel path all threw the same grey sprite. What it throws now is a piece of the ground it is standing on, which is what Weather2 Remastered's own debris particle does - it carries a `BlockState` rather than a sprite - and what GMod does with a broken prop: the gib is the object.

The piece is Weather2's own block particle, `ParticleCube`, which is not a new class to this mod: Weather2 draws its own flying blocks with it, and this mod's structure flight has drawn a lifted building with it since it was written, one cube per block at a scale of 0.5 - a half-extent that exactly fills the cell the block came from. Two things come with it that a sprite swap would not have got. It cuts its texture from a random *face* of the block rather than repeating the model's particle icon on every piece, so the faces of one chunk are not the same tile four times; and it carries the block's own `BlockColors` tint, so a grass chunk arrives green rather than grey. It is a different particle class rather than a different sprite for a reason that is easy to get wrong by hand: `ParticleCube` renders on the *block* atlas - its render type is Weather2's own `SORTED_OPAQUE_BLOCK`, which binds `TextureAtlas.LOCATION_BLOCKS` - where the funnel's own collar debris is a pivoting billboard on the particle sheet, and a block sprite's UVs mean nothing there.

A chunk is cut from the surface block under the point it is thrown from, through the same `surfaceState` column lookup the dust tone reads, so what flies is what the storm is standing on: grass and dirt over a scoured field, sand over a desert, gravel over a gravel path, stone over rock. Two cases are refused, because there is nothing there to tear: air, and a fluid. A column with no ground in it falls back to the stick it always threw, which is what a waterspout over open water has to throw, so the switch, its name and the whirls that read it are unchanged.

The sizes are the two figures the shipped libraries already set, and a cube's half-extent is its scale, so they can be compared directly. Weather2's own flying blocks use 0.45, nine tenths of a block; this mod's own structure flight fills the cell a block came from at 0.50. Loose debris is what was broken off a block rather than a block, so the band is 0.22 to 0.40 and no piece can arrive larger than the cell it was cut from. That band sits deliberately *below* the 0.30 to 0.58 the twig sprite drew in, and the reason is that a cube is all body where a twig was a soft blob inside its square: the area a piece actually paints is 0.19 to 0.64 of a block against the twig quad's 0.36 to 1.35, and even a chunk seen on its corner, showing two faces at once at 1.28, stays under the largest twig quad. The field can only have got lighter as its pieces got solid, which is the claim the harness measures rather than the band being chosen by eye.

Nothing about the flight changed. The wind still carries it at a weight of 1.2, it still tumbles at 8 to 22 degrees a tick, it still falls at a gravity of 0.045, it is still visual only through anything it hits, it still expires on the same 26-to-49 tick clock, and it is still told it is solid - the cube renders on the opaque block sheet and its render never reads an alpha at all, so `setAlpha(1.0F)` is a statement rather than a knob. All five spawn sites still throw through the one spawner - `spawnSatelliteSwirls`, `spawnMainTornadoStickDebris`, `spawnSubVortexStickDebris`, `spawnAddonWeatherEvents` and `spawnSquallLineVisual`, which between them cover the tornado, its sub-vortices, satellites, landspouts, dust devils, waterspouts and squall winds - under the one switch and behind the one per-tick budget of 80 pieces.

What the change retired is the machinery that existed to fake locality with a tint: the twig spawner, the dust-coat tone beside it, the twenty-eight-block dryness table under that, and `randomDebrisSprite`, which turned out to have no callers at all and was dead before this change touched it. What it did *not* retire is the two debris sheets. Weather2's own debris still reads `debris_1/2/3` off its registry, and the layered funnel's own collar still draws them, because that collar is a pivoting particle riding the particle sheet - which is exactly the atlas constraint above. So the art switch still resolves all six sheets and still writes all six registry fields, and the harness asserts that rather than assuming it.

`.freebuff/scratch/GroundChunkSim.java` holds all of it in 42 checks, on the source alone, from the project root. The band is not written down in the harness: it is *read out of the source*, and the arithmetic is then done on what it read, so a constant that is moved is measured rather than matched. Beyond the band and the painted-area comparison it checks the cut - the column lookup, both refusals, the gate, the size coming from the named constants rather than a literal - the flight, all six figures above, the coverage (five spawn sites, five density gates, three switch reads, the budget held), and the two readers that still need the sheets.

Twenty-eight deliberate breaks are each caught, and the tree is restored byte-identical afterwards. They come in five kinds. The band: widened past Weather2's own flying block, opened out to exactly it, collapsed to one size, given a zero, and renamed out from under the arithmetic that reads it. The cut: the size left as a literal, the column lookup replaced by the block at the debris' own height, which is air, the air and the fluid refusals removed one at a time, and the extended-renderer gate opened. The flight: the wind, the tumble, the gravity, the collision flags, the age clock and the alpha each moved to a plausible-looking wrong value, which is the kind of edit a later tuning pass makes without reading this section. The retirement: the twig helper put back as a real method, and the loose field picking a sheet again. And the two readers, the coverage and the budget: the router's resolve and its registry write, the funnel collar's own draw, one whirl no longer throwing through the spawner, one density gate dropped, one switch read dropped, and the budget no longer held.

Two misses on the first run were both the harness's own, and the first is the subtler one. The budget comparison it looked for is stated twice in the file - once in the spawner and once in a whirl's own gate - so a search of the whole file could not tell a budget that is held from one that is merely mentioned somewhere else; it reads the spawner's body now. The second was a fallback made unreachable by a `return` planted above it, which no search of the text can see, because the line is still there and still after the chunk attempt. The harness now requires the spawner to have exactly two ways out - the budget, and a chunk that landed - which is the shape that makes the stick line what is left when neither did rather than merely present.

Three honest notes. I cannot play this, so whether an opaque cube reads as debris rather than as a block flying is a session's verdict: the numbers here say what a piece is made of and how much of the screen it paints, not how it reads. The five spawn sites are one method, so the loose field changed everywhere at once, which is why the painted-area comparison is the load-bearing claim rather than a spot check on one whirl. And one split is deliberate and left standing: the funnel's own collar debris still draws the two sheets, because converting it means either dropping the pivot that keeps it orbiting the funnel or putting a block sprite on the particle sheet, and each of those is its own pass rather than a line in this one.

## The sea at a beach: what the waves were doing to the sand

The ocean surface is drawn from a lattice of corner samples, one quad per cell, and everything that happens where the water meets land is arithmetic on the water's depth. Three things in that arithmetic were wrong, and the smallest of them was the one the feature's own harness had been printing all along.

The first is the run-up, the film of water that runs up the sand as a crest arrives and drains back between them. A shore is made of blocks, so the first row of sand past the water stands a whole block above the still-water line - and the reach was measured from that line. The film can only be painted on the top of a row of sand, so the reach had to clear a whole block of sand before any of it could lie on any sand at all, and on top of that the reach was scaled to a reference swell of 0.9 blocks of crest, which is a 58 mph wind. Measured on the shipped numbers, the rule could not wet the row above the waterline in any wind below **47 mph**: a calm sea wet the waterline and nothing above it, a 40 mph storm wet the waterline and nothing above it, and only a gale began to wash the row a player's eye is actually on. `SurfSim` had been printing that as `run-up on the first sand row: peak 0.00, drained 0.00` in every mode it was run in, and it had no check for it, which is why the number could sit there being zero.

The reach now has two terms. The step the water is standing against is climbed by the swell's own share of a block, and the knob - `surfRunUpBlocks`, the same one, the same meaning to the player - is spent on the beach above that step. On a beach that steps one block per block the ladder comes out as one a player can read: at the sea's own floor of 8 mph the waterline row is wet and the row above it is dry; at 45 mph the wash climbs that row; at 60 mph it wets it right across a crest and it is dry in the trough behind; at 90 mph a second row is reached; and at the top of the wind, where the sea is against its amplitude cap, the third row gets a hint and no more. The wind that first wets the row above the waterline comes down from 48 mph to 34, and the drain is still there at every row: every row the wash touches is wetter behind a crest than in the trough it left, which is what makes a run-up read as water arriving rather than as a painted band.

Two more things came out of the same pass, and both were in the same twenty lines. The film was being laid on any ground the swash could reach *downward* as well as upward - the test was `above > reach`, so a sample of seabed a step below the waterline, whose top is below the still-water plane, counted as sand the water could run up, and got a film of whitewater painted on it under the water it was already beneath. Only ground strictly above the line takes a film now; ground at or below it is dropped with its colour, so no quad is drawn into it. And the whole of the run-up and of the surf's foam is now behind the surf switch and behind the knob: `surfEnabled` off leaves no breakers, no whitewater and no wash, and a run-up of 0 leaves the water entirely, which is what both of them have always said they do. Previously the switch stood down the breaking and the blackwater and left the wash on the sand regardless.

The second defect is the band of water around land whose depth the lattice is told about, and it is the bigger of the two by eye. Both of the things the surface does near a shore - a wave growing as the water shallows, and being cut back to the height the water can hold - are functions of the depth, and the depth is only measured for the corners inside that band. Everything outside it is drawn as open water. The band was sized in advance: the influence depth over the cell size, in rings. That is the shallow-water band of a bottom that drops one whole cell per cell, and most shores are not that. On a bottom sloping one block down per four blocks out, the influence depth of a big sea is 7 blocks and the old band ended at 8 blocks of beach, where the water is 2 blocks deep: outside it the swell stood at its full 2.2 blocks while the last probed corner was cut back to 0.78, and the step between them was **0.42 of a block at 70 mph and 1.42 blocks at the top of the wind** - a ring of raised water all the way around the beach, cutting across the surf zone and the swell alike, in step with nothing in the world. The band now grows out from the land one ring at a time until a whole ring comes back deeper than the waves can care about, so the number of rings is a property of the seabed rather than of the lattice: 2 rings on a steep shore (the same as before, so a cliff coast costs nothing extra), 4 on a shore sloping one in two, and 8 - where the cap bites - on one sloping one in four, with the edge of the band at 8 blocks of depth against the 7.05 the largest sea can still feel. The cliff on an ordinary shore measures 0.64 of a block with the old band and 0.00 with the new.

The third is small and is about movement rather than about shape. Each cell draws one window of the still-water sprite, drawn from a hash of its indices, so that a sea made of identical stretched quads does not read as a grid. That hash was keyed to the *lattice slot* - one index and one world coordinate added together - while the lattice itself is rebuilt the moment the camera swims or sails across a cell. A cell's window therefore changed every time the lattice moved, and the whole surface's texture popped forward in step with nothing a player can see. It is keyed to the cell's place in the world now, so a patch of sea draws the same window wherever the lattice happens to be standing.

`.freebuff/scratch/OceanWaveSim.java` holds all of it in 73 checks, and it is written the way the art and debris harnesses are: every number it uses - the beach step, the run-up scales and reference, the film, the ring cap, the probe cap, the sea's amplitude, wavelength, wind, steepness, the shoal depth, the break ratio, the gain, the knob's default and both switches - is read out of the shipped sources, so a constant changed in the renderer or a default moved in the config breaks the check that guards its meaning. It measures the ladder wind by wind, measures the rule being replaced the same way so that the fix is a comparison rather than a claim, sweeps every mile an hour from 1 to 200 for monotonicity, checks that the reach stays inside the knob's own ceiling, checks that no row under the waterline can take a film at any sea state, checks the window's stability under a lattice that shifts by a cell four ways over, and models four seabeds to measure the cliff the old band left and the cliff the new one does not. `SurfSim` now carries the shipped rule and, more to the point, carries the checks it was missing: the wash reaches the first sand row above the water in a storm's sea, drains back off it between crests, stays dry in a breeze, and the sand stays dry with the surf off. `ShoreGapSim` is unchanged and still passes, since the shoreline's gap to the water's own edge and the seam where the refined cells meet the lattice are not part of this.

Thirty-seven deliberate breaks - the beach step dropped to nothing and claimed as two blocks, the step climbed in full at every sea state, the knob's reach frozen at the swell's floor and spent unscaled, the reference swell moved up to the amplitude cap, both scales moved out, the film lifted into a step, the surf switch and the run-up knob each loosened, the ground under the waterline allowed a sample again, the reach test loosened, the plane subtracted the wrong way, the film dropped onto the sand itself, the wash's alpha and its whitewater flattened, the waterline flattened into a fade, the wash switched on instead of animated, the window keyed to the slot again, the hash no longer halved, both axes given the same window, the band grown on a ring that says nothing and on a shallow ring, its growth loop bounded by a literal, its cap moved out of range and its probe cap opened past the depth the surface is told apart by, the surf's foam taken out from behind the switch, and nine of the config's own defaults moved - are each caught by a measurement of the shipped sources, with the tree restored byte-identical afterwards. The first run of the sweep found one miss and it was the harness's fault rather than the break's: a hurricane flooding a row the knob never named passed the "does not flood the beach" check, because coverage is a share of a row and the denominator grows with the row, so the check now counts the rows the biggest sea wets instead. `SurfSim`, `ShoreGapSim` and `OceanWaveSim` join the standing set, which is twenty-eight harnesses.

Three honest notes. I cannot watch the sea, so everything above is arithmetic on the shipped constants rather than a look at a shoreline: whether the wash *reads* as water running up sand - a whitewater band that arrives and drains, rather than paint - is a session's verdict and is the one claim here that no measurement settles. The band grows until the ring it just probed is deep, which is the right answer for a shore and can miss a shallow patch sitting outside a ring of deep water; a reef four or five cells offshore is drawn as open water, and the ring cap of eight means a bottom that never reaches the influence depth within 32 blocks keeps a step of up to 0.12 of a block, which is where the "only step left is the cap's own" line in the harness comes from. And one meaning has changed under an unchanged name: `surfRunUpBlocks` is now how far the wash runs up *from the top of the block the water is standing against*, rather than from the still-water line, so a beach that steps a block per block gets a row more wash than it used to for the same number. The shipped default of 1.5 was chosen against that reading and is what the ladder above is measured at; a config file carrying a custom value should be re-read against it.

## Eclipses under a shader pack

The eclipse used to be the one thing in this mod that a shader pack simply deleted. Not by a bug - by an explicit stand-down: with a pack active the mod hands the whole sky over, because the pack draws its own sun and moon and the mod's own sky program is one of the ones a pack replaces, so leaving the mod's own sky armed would have been a second sun drawn with whatever program the pack put in its place. What that also removed was the eclipse itself, which is not a second sun. It is a body crossing a body, plus the darkening of everything around it, and the darkening is the half that does not need the sky pass at all.

So the darkening moved into the reading the rest of the mod already uses. `StormSky.eclipseDarkness` folds the eclipse into the same number a storm's deck produces - the value vanilla's own sky-darken is built from - and `StormSkyClient.darkness()` multiplies the two together. One reading, four surfaces, exactly as a deck has: the sky's colour, the cloud deck's, the fog's, and the world's own ambient light through `getSkyDarken`, which is the value the lightmap is generated from. That is why it survives a pack: a pack can take the sky program, but it cannot take `getSkyDarken`, because that is what the lightmap and every baked light in the world are drawn from. A total eclipse now dims an afternoon under Complementary the same way it dims one under the game. The two shares compose (a deck over an eclipse is darker than either), the result is held on vanilla's own midnight floor so nothing asks a renderer for a value it has no case for, and the eclipse's own half is behind `eclipseDarkness` - switch that off and the eclipse is a black disc on a bright afternoon, exactly as before.

The mod's own sun and moon still stand down under a pack - there is only ever one sun, and this does not change that. What is new is `eclipseUnderShaderPacks`, off by default, which lets the mod try to draw the eclipse's own pieces (the black disc, its corona and the diamond ring) on top of a pack's sky as well. It is off because a pack that does not provide a program for the mod's celestial shader draws these with its fallback instead, which may not appear at all or may look wrong; the darkening is the half that is guaranteed, and it happens with or without the switch. The renderer's own fog pass is gone - the eclipse is part of the shared reading now, and two places darkening the same fog is one too many.

`.freebuff/scratch/LodStormSim.java` checks the wiring of both halves of this update - the eclipse's darkening being part of the shared reading and its overlay shipping off - alongside the storm range below, and `.freebuff/scratch/CelestialSim.java` (179 checks) and `IrisCompatSim.java` (24, against the Iris jar that is actually installed) were updated to the new contracts and re-run clean. One honest note: I cannot launch a pack, so that `getSkyDarken` reaches a given pack's lighting the way it reaches the game's own is read from how the value is consumed rather than watched on a screen; whether a particular pack *shows* it is that pack's business, and the overlay switch is there for the packs that do not.

## Storms out over LOD terrain

The rendering compat fixed the weather that was already there - a funnel for the terrain an LOD renderer draws, fog that does not end short of it - and left the weather it had not made yet. Weather2 spawns its storms inside a box around the players (`Misc_simBoxRadiusSpawn`, 1024 blocks shipped) and deletes a storm after ten seconds with no player within `Misc_simBoxRadiusCutoff` (1124), so with Distant Horizons or Voxy drawing thousands of blocks the sky over most of the visible country was permanently clear. Nothing can be spawned there by a rendering fix, because the storm is a server object and the renderer is a client.

So the box is widened, at the two reads that size it. Both are redirects on Weather2's own `ConfigMisc` statics - the scan that forgets a distant storm, and the four reads that draw a spawn point - and both answer with `LodStormEnvelope`, which returns Weather2's configured number untouched unless the compat is on, the storm extension is on, and an LOD mod is actually installed. It never narrows a box a world already had, and with no LOD renderer it is a no-op down to the value. The distance is one number in one place: `stormDistanceBlocks` when it is set, otherwise the same LOD view distance the rendering compat uses, otherwise the configured ceiling. The spawn offset stays triangular about the player, so most storms still form near somebody and some form out over the far country - which is the point.

The storms also have to be *seen*, and that is the second half. Weather2 sends its storm objects to every client in the dimension, so a storm that exists is already drawn inside the renderer's cull - extended by the rendering compat - but this mod's own storm state (the circulation, its satellites, its sub-vortices) is pushed only within `multiplayer.syncRangeBlocks`, which would leave a distant funnel wearing its default sliver. That range is widened with the box, so the storm and its state reach the same player.

The third piece is the mod's own whirls. A dust devil or a snow devil was refused the instant its chunk was not loaded, which means never over LOD-only terrain - and the `isLoaded` check was there for a real reason, since reading a block in an unloaded chunk loads it, and loading a chunk to ask a cosmetic question is the cost the gate avoided. So the unloaded branch does not read blocks at all: it reads the biome, which is available without loading anything. Dry families for a dust devil, a snow devil's frozen evidence taken from the biome's own ability to snow, rain and open sky falling back to the level's flag. A loaded chunk is judged exactly as it always was, block by block.

`.freebuff/scratch/LodStormSim.java` holds all of it in 33 checks: the config's one-distance rule and its defaults, the envelope never narrowing and needing an LOD renderer, both mixin redirects and their exact targets and expected counts, the addon's unloaded branches and the absence of the old gates, and then the reflected half - it loads the Weather2 jar that is actually installed and asks it whether the class, both fields and both methods the redirects name are really there, because a redirect onto a name that does not exist builds, loads and does nothing. `Phase6Sim` (171), `SnowDevilSim` (127), `ClimateSim` (167), `CalendarSim` (181), `DebrisSim` (107) and the rest of the standing set were re-run clean. Two honest notes. I cannot run a world, so how far out the weather *looks* right is a session's verdict; and a widened box with the same spawn chance does thin the storms near a player a little, which is why the distance defaults to the renderer's rather than to something larger - set `stormDistanceBlocks` lower if you want the weather hugging you while the LODs draw far away.
